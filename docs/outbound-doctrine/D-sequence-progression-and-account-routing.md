# Handbook D — Sequence progression and account routing

**What this handbook governs:** how a *sequence* advances over time, and how many
people at one account you contact and in what order. It stops follow-ups from
repeating the same hypothesis and stops the generator from blasting five inboxes
with the same body.

**Failure modes it targets:** #4 repeating the same hypothesis across follow-ups,
#6 contacting several people at one company with no routing strategy.

**Primary sources:** [04] Holland (build a sequence), [08] Allen-Knuth
(enterprise routing), with support from [06] 30MPC, [07] 30MPC discovery, [09]
SPICED, [10] Dunford, [02] Holland.

---

## The follow-up value gate (governing hard rule)

> **Reject any follow-up that does not add new evidence, a more concrete example,
> a useful artifact or a materially different ask.**

A follow-up is not a reminder. "The difference between spam and persistence is
personalization" — frequency is only earned when every touch carries its own
specific reason. Before a follow-up is stored it must pass one of these four,
and it must not restate a prior touch's hypothesis, question, or CTA:

1. **New evidence** — a verified fact learned since (a hire, a post, a number, a
   regulation, an event). [04][01]
2. **A more concrete example** — a real, named-outcome peer case or a sharper
   illustration of the same problem, scoped honestly (Handbook A). [08][10]
3. **A useful artifact** — something specific and usable without a meeting (a
   one-screen sketch, a rough cost model, a sample record). Delivered under the
   no-link rule. [07][10]
4. **A materially different ask** — a new, role-relevant question or a routing
   question, not the same request reworded. [08][07]

If none of the four is genuinely present, do not send it. [04]

## Sequence progression

1. **Prepare at least three distinct premises up front** so later touches rotate
   to a genuinely different angle instead of echoing the first. [04]

2. **Each touch advances the SPICED stage or switches to a new, named angle.**
   Use the prior (non-)answer to build the next question; do not re-interrogate
   the same point ("discovery fatigue"). [09][07]

3. **Every touch still leads with the reason before any value or solution**, and
   still resolves to exactly one low-effort CTA. Adding pressure is not adding
   value. [04][06]

4. **Do not grow more confident than the evidence over time.** More touches must
   not silently turn a hypothesis into an assertion; the uncertainty framing
   (Handbook B) persists across the whole sequence. [09][10]

5. **Coordinate channels around one argument.** A LinkedIn touch or a threaded
   reply should reinforce the same case, not restart it. Reply on the existing
   thread to continue an argument; start a new email only for a genuinely new
   premise. Acknowledge a prior channel ("I sent this over email, but…") rather
   than pretending the first touch didn't happen. [04][02]

6. **End with an honest close, and honor it.** A breakup/closing touch that says
   you'll stop must actually be followed by stopping. No guilt, no fake deadline,
   no "last chance." [04]

## Account routing

7. **Pick the first contact by likely problem ownership, not seniority.** The
   operator/user who performs the workflow feels the pain; the senior title often
   does not. Pitching an economic owner a line-level workflow problem lands flat.
   [08][03]

8. **Single-thread first when the owner is unconfirmed.** With no confirmed
   problem and no confirmed owner, contact exactly one person. Multi-threading
   here is premature and reads as a blast. [08]

9. **Never send two or more contacts at one account the same body.** If you
   contact more than one person, vary by that person's role and what they can
   credibly answer. [08][02][06]

10. **Match the ask to the role.** The operator/user confirms whether the
    workflow and its difficulty are real; the router/champion confirms who else
    is affected and routes you inward; the economic owner speaks to consequence,
    budget, and priority — not shift-level detail. [08][03]

11. **Reuse ONE company-level trigger across an account, routed per role.** A
    single public trigger (a new office, a funding round) can anchor the account;
    give each contact a role-appropriate angle off it, sequenced, not simultaneous
    and not identical. [06]

12. **Ask a routing question as a correctable guess, not an open "who owns
    this?"** "It looked like your team owns X — is that you, or someone else?"
    Correcting a specific guess is far less work than mapping their own org. [08]

13. **Reach a second stakeholder deliberately, only after a first contact
    confirms the problem and points you there** — with a role-tailored,
    problem-first note, never the recycled cold email. Let an engaged group
    surface "who should have been here that wasn't," then get introduced. [08]

14. **Route to the likely champion first; give non-champions objection-handling,
    not a value pitch.** People who cannot create the deal can still kill it;
    arm the champion to handle them rather than pitching value to IT/finance in
    parallel. [10]

## Common failure patterns

- A follow-up that restates the prior hypothesis reworded ("just circling back —
  still think X is hurting you?"). [04][07]
- Growing more certain, or introducing solution/ROI, simply because it's a later
  touch. [09][10]
- Fading out — heavy early, then trailing off — which teaches the prospect that
  waiting you out works. [04]
- Blasting the whole account: the same body to five people at once, with no first
  contact confirming the problem. [08]
- Choosing the first contact by seniority rather than problem ownership. [08]
- Pitching the economic buyer a workflow problem they don't personally feel. [08]
- An open "who owns this?" that makes the recipient map their own org. [08]
- Pitching value to non-champions instead of handing objection-handling to a
  champion. [10]

## Bad / good example pairs

- **Bad (follow-up):** "As I mentioned, I think our tool could help your team."
  **Good (follow-up):** "Different angle from my first note — saw your comment on
  onboarding ramp; that's actually the thread I was pulling on. Is ramp time the
  bigger pain than the reporting I raised first?"
  **Why:** rotates to a second, pre-identified premise and a new ask rather than
  echoing the first. [04]

- **Bad:** the same email sent at 9am to the CEO, VP Ops, plant manager, and two
  supervisors.
  **Good:** email only the plant manager (most likely to feel the workflow pain);
  if they confirm and name the VP as the funder, ask for an intro and send the VP
  a consequence-framed note.
  **Why:** replaces a simultaneous account-wide blast with a routed, single-
  threaded start that fans out deliberately. [08]

- **Bad (to the CFO):** "Not sure if your welders are struggling with the new
  weld-tracking checklist each shift?"
  **Good (to the CFO):** "Another manufacturer found welder turnover was quietly
  adding cost to their megaproject timeline — open to hearing what they changed?"
  **Why:** the CFO can speak to consequence and budget, not a shift-level
  checklist; match the ask to the role. [08]

- **Bad:** "Who owns weld scheduling over there?"
  **Good:** "It seemed like weld scheduling sits with your ops team — is that you,
  or should I be talking to someone else?"
  **Why:** correcting a specific guess is a low-effort reply that also routes you.
  [08]

## Conditions under which these rules apply

- **Single-thread first** is the default for cold accounts; **multi-thread only
  once** a champion has confirmed the problem, indicated others are affected, and
  ideally offered an introduction. [08]
- **Reuse-one-trigger** applies to multi-contact accounts; a single-contact touch
  still needs the fact-to-relevance hook but no routing. [06][02]
- The **exact cadence** (Holland's 16 touches / shrinking gaps) is a tunable
  template, not a mandate; the transferable rules are: distinct premise per new
  email, one CTA per email, don't fade, honor the breakup. Our motion is
  low-volume and human-approved, so scale the absolute touch count *down* — the
  personalization/new-value gate is the binding constraint, not touch count. [04]
- **Channel coordination:** a threaded reply continues one argument; a new email
  signals a new idea. Phone/voicemail steps in the source are out of scope for an
  email + LinkedIn motion; adopt only the intent. [04]
- The full positioning arc across touches (Handbook C) and the discovery tree
  (Handbook B) are what each touch advances *through*. [10][07]

## Evaluation checklist

1. Does this follow-up pass the value gate — new evidence, a more concrete
   example, a useful artifact, or a materially different ask? → else do not send.
2. Does it repeat a prior touch's hypothesis, question, or CTA? → fail.
3. Does confidence stay within the evidence (no new assertion just because it's a
   later touch)? → else fail.
4. Are 2+ contacts at one account getting the same body? → fail.
5. Is a second stakeholder being contacted before any first contact confirmed the
   problem and pointed there? → fail.
6. Is the first contact chosen by likely problem ownership rather than seniority?
   → else fail.
7. Is any routing question a correctable guess rather than an open "who owns
   this?" → else revise.
8. For an economic-owner touch, is the ask scoped to consequence/priority, not
   shift-level workflow detail? → else fail.
9. Does a closing touch commit to stopping — and does the plan actually stop
   afterward? → else fail.
