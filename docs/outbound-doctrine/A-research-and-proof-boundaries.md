# Handbook A — Research and proof boundaries

**What this handbook governs:** what a piece of research is allowed to *claim*.
It draws the line between a fact you observed and a workflow you inferred, and it
sets the scope of any proof (case study, statistic, peer example) the email uses.

**Failure modes it targets:** #1 turning a plausible hypothesis into an asserted
workflow, #2 inferring ownership from a title, #5 invented operational language.

**Primary sources:** [02] Holland (personalization → relevance), [01] Holland
(unknown problems), with support from [03] Migicovsky, [06] 30MPC/Gong, [07]
30MPC discovery, [09] SPICED, [10] Dunford.

---

## The governing hard rule

> **Public evidence may establish the company's environment, but it cannot
> establish the private workflow, its owner, its difficulty or its financial
> consequence.**

Everything below is an application of this one rule. Four things are *private*
and can only ever be a hypothesis until the recipient confirms them:

1. **The workflow** — how they actually do the thing today.
2. **The owner** — who performs it and who is accountable for it.
3. **The difficulty** — where it is hard, slow, manual, or error-prone.
4. **The financial consequence** — what it costs in money, hours, or risk.

Public evidence (a hire, a funding round, a new site, a post they wrote, an
approval, a tool in their stack) can establish only the **environment** in which
those four private things probably sit.

## Rules the generator must follow

1. **Classify every research item as an *observed fact* or an *inferred
   relevance* before using it.** A found fact ("they wrote a post on scaling by
   quality") is raw material; whether that fact makes the offer relevant is a
   separate inference that must be stated as such. Never let an observation stand
   in the copy as if its relevance were already proven. [02]

2. **Tag every field of the account brief with its evidence level.** Only the
   *situation* (environment) may be marked `confirmed` from public evidence. The
   workflow, its owner, its difficulty, and its cost start `unconfirmed` and stay
   that way until a reply promotes them. [07][09]

3. **Quote, or hypothesize — never assert.** If you have the person's own words
   (a post, a talk, a self-described trait, a public company statement), quote
   two or three of them and hook to them. If you do *not* have their words, frame
   the inference explicitly: "I'd imagine…", "correct me if I'm wrong…", "not
   sure if…". [02][08]

4. **Rank personalization by source quality, and never dress company data as
   personal.** Strongest to weakest: what they authored → what they engaged with
   → a self-identified trait → generic "junk-drawer" details → company-level
   news. Company-level facts are the weakest signal about an individual (except
   for owners), so do not imply a corporate event means the recipient personally
   feels anything. [02]

5. **Anchor the "reason for writing" to an observed action, not a title.** A
   funding round, an FDA approval, a hire, a new location, a talk they gave — a
   real event evidences something happened. A job title evidences nothing about
   ownership, pain, or workflow. [06][02]

6. **Use only real, sourced numbers, and frame them as benchmarks, not
   promises.** An industry average ("teams at this scale often…") can create
   useful context, but it must be a genuine sourced figure presented as a pattern
   — never a fabricated statistic and never applied to the named account as a
   measured fact about *them*. [01][06]

7. **Scope every proof point to exactly what it supports.** A customer case shows
   what happened *elsewhere*; it cannot promise the same result here, and it
   cannot establish that this recipient shares that private workflow. Name the
   correct outcome category (pipeline ≠ revenue ≠ hours ≠ reliability) and require
   authorization before naming a customer. [05][10][08]

8. **Build credibility from a fact the reader does not already know, not from
   tone.** Every line should either teach the reader something (a benchmark, a
   root cause, a peer's approach) or move the ask forward. Confidence, pace, and
   filler are not credibility. [01]

9. **Use the recipient's real vocabulary; never invent operational language.**
   Coined internal process names, invented metrics, or boardroom abstraction
   signal that the workflow was guessed. If you cannot name it in the buyer's own
   words or a plainly general term, do not name it. [03][10][06]

10. **Discard compliments and vague enthusiasm as evidence.** Only a specific,
    dated, concrete fact counts. "Love what you're building" is not a basis for
    asserting anything about their operation. [03]

## Common failure patterns

- Presenting a found fact as if its relevance to the offer were self-evident,
  with no stated hook. [02]
- Promoting a brief field (workflow / owner / difficulty / cost) to "confirmed"
  on public evidence alone. [07][09]
- Asserting what someone does when you only have an inference, instead of marking
  it "I'd imagine / correct me if I'm wrong." [02]
- Treating a corporate news item as if the individual recipient personally feels
  it. [02]
- Anchoring outreach to a title ("As VP of Ops you're struggling with…") rather
  than an observed event. [06][02]
- Quoting a statistic with no source, or applying an industry average to the
  named account as measured fact. [01][06]
- Stretching a case study into a promise, or naming an outcome category the proof
  does not support. [05][10]
- Inventing operational terminology to sound expert. [03][10]

## Bad / good example pairs

- **Bad:** "As VP of Operations, you're surely struggling to reconcile data
  across your systems."
  **Good:** "Saw you just got FDA approval to proceed with the FAPI trial.
  Operators at that stage often tell me startup gets slowed by double data entry
  across systems — is that live for your team, or already handled?"
  **Why:** replaces a title-inferred, asserted workflow with an observed public
  event plus an explicitly hypothesized difficulty. [06]

- **Bad:** "Your welders spend 40% of their day on rework, costing you about $X
  per unit."
  **Good:** "You're hiring MIG welders right now, and the shortage tends to push
  overtime and rework — is that showing up on your line?"
  **Why:** the public signal (hiring) is fair game; the private difficulty and
  its cost become a question, not a fabricated figure. [06][01]

- **Bad:** "You played D1 basketball, and so did I — let me tell you how
  disciplined I am."
  **Good:** "Correct me if I'm wrong, but D1 basketball probably came down to
  reps and technique. What if your team could raise call technique the same way?"
  **Why:** removes the self-focus, marks the inference as a hypothesis, and hooks
  the observed fact to something the recipient owns. [02]

- **Bad:** "We save comp teams like yours ~$120K a year."
  **Good:** "One team your size measured this and found the manual step was the
  expensive part — happy to share what they looked at."
  **Why:** the case establishes what happened elsewhere and is offered as
  information, not asserted as this account's guaranteed saving. [10][08]

## Conditions under which these rules apply

- These rules bind whenever **only public evidence** exists — the default before
  any reply. A recipient statement can promote any field from `unconfirmed` to
  `confirmed` at any time; after that, you may speak to it plainly. [07]
- The **quote-the-words** rule applies when you have on-record words; the
  **"I'd imagine"** framing applies to everything else (the junk-drawer case).
  [02]
- The **company-data-is-weak** caveat softens for a business **owner**, who may
  care about company-level events nearly as much as personal ones. [02]
- **Benchmark numbers** are allowed only when the figure is real and sourced, and
  only as an industry pattern — never as a measured claim about the named
  account. [01][06]
- **Proof** rules apply whenever a case study, quote, or peer is used; if no
  relevant, authorized proof exists, offer a sketch, sample, or bounded test
  instead of inventing one. [05][10]

## Evaluation checklist

Run these against any draft before it is stored:

1. Is every claim about the account either a cited public fact or explicitly
   marked as a hypothesis ("I'd imagine / not sure if / correct me")?
2. Is any private workflow, owner, difficulty, or dollar/hours figure asserted as
   fact on public evidence alone? → fail.
3. Is the "reason for writing" anchored to an observed event, not a job title?
4. Are all personalized facts hooked to why they matter, rather than left
   floating?
5. Is every statistic real, sourced, and framed as a benchmark rather than a
   promise about this account?
6. Does each proof point name the correct outcome category and stay within what
   it actually supports (with authorization for any named customer)?
7. Is the copy free of invented operational terms and boardroom abstraction — all
   in the recipient's own vocabulary?
8. Would every sentence teach the reader something they did not know or move the
   ask forward (no filler-as-credibility)?

  the five research buckets; quote-or-hypothesize; company-data is the weakest
  personal signal.
- **[01] Holland — unknown problems:** credibility = teaching an unknown fact;
  benchmarks as industry patterns, never promises.

- **[06] 30MPC / Gong:** activity-based anchors beat title; observed action as

- **[07] discovery / [09] SPICED:** only the situation (environment) may be

- **[10] Dunford:** an insight is an agreeable hypothesis, not an asserted private
