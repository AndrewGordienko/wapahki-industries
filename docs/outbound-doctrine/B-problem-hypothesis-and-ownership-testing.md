# Handbook B — Problem-hypothesis and ownership testing

**What this handbook governs:** how the email talks about the *problem* and *who
owns it*. It converts a plausible guess into a falsifiable hypothesis the
recipient can confirm or kill, keeps the qualification stages in order, and stops
the copy from inferring ownership or jumping to money.

**Failure modes it targets:** #1 asserting a workflow, #2 inferring ownership
from a title, #3 introducing solution/pilot/ROI before the problem is confirmed,
#7 making the recipient do too much work to answer.

**Primary sources:** [01] Holland (unknown problems), [03] Migicovsky (talk to
users), [07] 30MPC (discovery), [09] SPICED, with support from [02] Holland, [08]
Allen-Knuth, [10] Dunford.

---

## The core stance

A cold email does not *know* the recipient's problem. Its job is to propose a
**falsifiable hypothesis** and make it easy to confirm, correct, or reject. The
problem must be established before any consequence, ROI, solution, or pilot is
mentioned. The qualification stages have a fixed order, and copy may only speak
to the stages the evidence has actually reached.

**Qualification order (SPICED):** Situation → Pain → Impact → Critical event →
Decision. A cold email may normally touch only **Situation (evidenced)** plus
**one Pain hypothesis**. Impact, Critical event, and Decision are earned on a
reply or call — never asserted cold. Jumping Situation → Impact "sounds super
salesy" and inverts the sale. [09][07]

---

## Rules the generator must follow

1. **State the problem as a falsifiable hypothesis, and give the reader a way to
   disconfirm it.** Frame it as an industry pattern with a self-check ("teams at
   this scale often hit X — you'd know in one glance at your own numbers whether
   that's you"), not as a diagnosis of their internal workflow. [01][02]

2. **Use uncertainty language on anything private.** "Not sure if…", "I'd
   imagine…", "correct me if I'm wrong…", "is that live for you, or already
   solved?" People rebel against being told what they do; a tentative frame
   invites a correction, and a correction is a reply. [08][02][09]

3. **Do not assert or infer ownership from a title.** Ask who owns it, or route:
   "It looked like this sits with your team — is that you, or someone else?"
   Choose the *first* contact by who most plausibly performs or feels the
   workflow, not by seniority. [03][08][02]

4. **Ground the question in the last real occurrence, not a hypothetical.** Ask
   "when this last happened, what made it hard?" not "would you use a tool that…"
   Hypothetical and would-you-pay questions produce fluff and smuggle the solution
   in before the problem is confirmed. [03]

5. **Confirm the problem before the consequence.** Do not state or quantify
   Impact (a dollar figure, ROI, "saves X hours") in a cold email. At most, point
   at a metric or risk the recipient can confirm — never assert it. Financial
   impact is the private consequence public evidence cannot establish. [07][09]

6. **Withhold solution, product, pilot, demo, and peer-story until a reply
   confirms the problem.** The first email's job is to learn and to earn a
   correction, not to sell. Leading with the fix makes the reader discount it as
   an advertisement. [03][07][10][06]

7. **Offer a bounded choice instead of an open interrogation.** Give one or two
   explicit options to pick or reject ("is it the time in spreadsheets, the lack
   of a check on decisions, or neither?"). This steers toward where the offer
   actually helps and makes the reply one line. [07][01]

8. **Keep the ask answerable from memory in one line, with "no" made easy.** A
   busy person should be able to confirm, correct, or decline without composing a
   narrative or doing research. Fast disqualification is a win. [03][07][01]

9. **Uncover a new layer of the problem on each follow-up, never the same
   hypothesis.** A problem has distinct parts — root cause, current impact, future
   impact, a trigger. Widen the reader's *perspective* with a new layer; do not
   restate the known one louder. (This is the follow-up value gate; see Handbook
   D.) [01][07][03]

10. **Frame any emotion or difficulty as a hypothesized feeling tied to a cited
    situation, not an asserted experience.** "That stage can get frustrating" is
    allowed as a guess; "you're overwhelmed by X" is an assertion. [09][02]

11. **Anchor the problem to the metric the persona is actually measured on.** If
    the pain does not move the one number this role is judged on, they will not
    care. Do not pitch an outcome the recipient does not own. [02][01]

## Common failure patterns

- Asserting the private workflow as fact ("your team manually reconciles X every
  week") instead of proposing it as a hypothesis with a self-check. [01][07]
- Jumping Situation → Impact: seeing a public fact and immediately stating a
  business or dollar consequence. [09]
- Quantifying anything (dollars, ROI, hours, percentages) before the problem is
  confirmed. [07][09]
- Naming a Critical Event or Decision process/owner that is not in cited public
  evidence — fabricated urgency. [09]
- Inferring ownership or accountability from a job title. [03][08][02]
- Asking a hypothetical ("would you use…", "would you pay…") instead of about a
  real past event. [03]
- Introducing solution, pilot, ROI, or a peer story before the problem is agreed.
  [03][07][10]
- Asking an open, high-effort question ("walk me through your whole process")
  that forces the recipient to do the diagnostic work. [07][03]

## Bad / good example pairs

- **Bad:** "You round-robin your inbound and make prospects wait until Monday,
  which is costing you deals."
  **Good:** "This usually shows up when three things are true — leads get
  round-robined, prospects can't self-schedule, and demos wait for Monday. If
  that's your setup it may be worth a look; if not, ignore me."
  **Why:** turns an asserted workflow into a litmus test the reader checks
  themselves. [01]

- **Bad:** "As Head of Ops you own scheduling, and it's clearly your biggest
  bottleneck."
  **Good:** "Your ops group scaled fast this year. I'm not sure where
  shift-scheduling actually sits — is that your team, and is it a pain point
  yet?"
  **Why:** stops inferring ownership and pain from a title; makes both a
  question. [02][08]

- **Bad:** "Would your team use an automated routing tool if we built one?"
  **Good:** "Thinking of the last routing decision that went sideways — what had
  you already tried to stop it?"
  **Why:** replaces a hypothetical about a future product with a real past event
  that yields actual signal. [03]

- **Bad:** "This is costing you roughly $2M a year — worth a 20-minute pilot?"
  **Good:** "Before I say anything about a fix — is reconciliation friction even
  worth your time this quarter, or a nice-to-have?"
  **Why:** no dollar figure and no pilot before the problem is confirmed; the
  push-pull permits a clean "no." [07][09]

- **Bad (follow-up 2):** "Just circling back — bet you're still losing hours to
  manual reconciliation."
  **Good (follow-up 2):** "My last note assumed the manual step was the pain and
  may be off. The other thing I see at your stage is oversight — managers making
  off-policy calls with no easy check. Closer, or not it?"
  **Why:** advances to a new layer and re-tests, rather than repeating the first
  guess. [01][07]

## Conditions under which these rules apply

- **Cold first touch:** Situation (cited) + at most one Pain hypothesis + one
  low-effort confirming ask. No asserted Impact, no dollar figure, no Critical
  Event, no Decision mapping, no solution/pilot. [09][07]
- **After a reply confirms the problem:** you may explore Impact (rational,
  emotional, financial), test whether a real Critical Event exists ("test the
  date — no consequence means it isn't critical"), and introduce the solution and
  a next step. Quantification and pilot language become permitted only here.
  [09][07]
- **Ownership:** ask or route whenever the owner is unconfirmed; address a role
  directly only when they plausibly perform or feel the workflow. [08][03]
- **The unknown-problem framing** fits offers that solve a problem buyers usually
  don't know they have; if the buyer is already in-market and self-educated,
  naming the known pain directly is fine. [01]
- **One tree per product / persona / situation** — do not force unrelated
  situations into one hypothesis or one email. [07]

## Evaluation checklist

1. Is the problem framed as a falsifiable hypothesis (with uncertainty language),
   not an asserted workflow? → else fail.
2. Does the email assert Impact, a dollar/ROI/hours figure, or a percentage
   before the problem is confirmed? → fail.
3. Does it name a Critical Event or Decision owner/committee not backed by cited
   evidence? → fail.
4. Is ownership asked about or routed, rather than inferred from a title? → else
   fail.
5. Does the main question point at a real, specific past event (not a hypothetical
   or a "would you use…")? → else fail.
6. Are solution, pilot, demo, and peer-story absent until a reply confirms the
   problem? → else fail.
7. Can the recipient confirm, correct, or decline in one line, with "no" made
   easy? → else fail.
8. Does the problem connect to the metric this persona is actually measured on?
9. For a follow-up, does it add a new layer of the problem rather than repeat the
   prior hypothesis? → else fail.

## Per-source contribution

- **[01] Holland — unknown problems:** lead with an unknown problem, not a known
  pain; falsifiable hypothesis + self-check litmus test; defer the solution;
  widen perspective, don't exaggerate.
- **[03] Migicovsky — talk to users:** "tell me about the last time"; no
  hypotheticals or would-you-pay questions; test budget/authority rather than
  assume it; compliments aren't data.
- **[07] 30MPC — discovery:** the situation → operational → executive → impact
  tree; point at consequence, never quantify cold; problem-owner not title;
  bounded multiple-choice asks.
- **[09] SPICED:** the S→P→I→C→E→D order; Impact is earned, not asserted; test the
  date on any Critical Event.
- **[02] Holland / [08] Allen-Knuth:** unsure tonality invites correction;
  ownership comes from evidence, not seniority.
- **[10] Dunford:** the insight is an agreeable hypothesis; defer pilot/ROI until
  the problem is confirmed.
