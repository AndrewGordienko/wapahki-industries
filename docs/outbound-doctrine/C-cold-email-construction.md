# Handbook C — Cold-email construction

**What this handbook governs:** the line-level build of a single email — its
order, length, language, and its one ask. It assumes the problem hypothesis
(Handbook B) and the evidence boundary (Handbook A) are already right; its job is
to render them so the email is easy to read on a phone and trivial to answer.

**Failure modes it targets:** #5 invented / seller-centric language, #7 making
the recipient do too much work to answer, and the premature-pitch half of #3.

**Primary sources:** [05] Allred (Lavender rewrite), [06] 30MPC/Gong (85M
emails), [10] Dunford (positioning), with support from [03] Migicovsky, [02][01]
Holland.

---

## The canonical structure (the "reply method")

Order every first-touch email so each sentence earns the next:

1. **Reason (why them):** an observed fact about the recipient's world — a
   question about their current state, or an event. Not the sender, not the
   product. [05][06]
2. **Problem:** one Pain hypothesis in the buyer's own operational terms, framed
   as a hypothesis (Handbook B). [06]
3. **Value / light social proof:** only if real and attributable, and only the
   outcome that matters to this persona — not a feature list. [06][05][10]
4. **One CTA:** a single, low-effort, answerable ask. [06][05]

Positioning comes *before* product: frame the problem and at least one real
alternative the buyer is weighing before naming the offer. State the offer only
after that, as category-plus-differentiator, never as a capability dump. [10]

## Rules the generator must follow

1. **Open about the recipient, not the sender or the product.** Make the first
   sentence a question about their current state or an observed event. Cut any
   "I have a product relevant to your work / you need this" opener — "nothing says
   I don't need it like a sales rep telling me I need it." [05][06]

2. **Keep it short and skimmable: roughly 50–100 words, 3–4 sentences, in short
   blocks.** The first open is far more likely on a phone, and 50 unbroken words
   become a wall of text. Break the quote or key line onto its own line. Treat the
   numbers as a data-backed default to test, not a law. [06][05]

3. **Exactly one ask.** Do not stack a second question, a migration term, pricing,
   an attachment to review, or "a long-term relationship." Every extra clause is
   work moved onto the reader. [06][04]

4. **Make the CTA offer-based or interest-based, never a time/meeting request in a
   first touch.** In the 85M-email data, meeting/time asks reduced replies most
   and offer-based asks performed best. Prefer "want me to send the short
   version?" or "is this interesting, or not really?" over "do you have 15
   minutes Friday?" [06][05]

5. **Let a smart question carry the context; do not describe the product.** A good
   question orients the reader without a product paragraph. Name the offer only
   after the problem and an alternative are framed, as what-it-is-plus-what's-
   different. [05][10]

6. **Translate every capability into its value with a "so what?" — and cut the
   capability if it has none.** Buyers cannot tell overlapping features apart;
   they buy the outcome the alternatives can't deliver. Never open on a
   feature list or a generic macro trend. [10]

7. **Attach every number to a cause.** A bare statistic is forgettable; pair it
   with the reason it happened ("they were losing hours to manual rework — 10+ a
   week"). Give any quote its attribution and setup *before* the quote. [05]

8. **Ban buzzwords and product-pitch vocabulary.** No "optimize / streamline /
   accelerate / seamless / single source of truth / platform / leverage /
   unlock," and do not open by talking about "AI" or your solution — in the data
   these depress replies. Use plain, priority-based language the buyer already
   uses. [06][10]

9. **Never use FOMO or pressure.** "Your competitors already do this / don't fall
   behind" makes an indecisive buyer *more* likely to do nothing. Reduce perceived
   risk instead (start with the smallest slice). [10]

10. **Read every line aloud; if you would not say it to the person, cut it.** A
    jarring jump to "when's good for a call?" is awkward out loud; a wall of
    hedges is too. Each sentence must logically drive the next. [05]

11. **Personalize the subject and keep it non-marketing.** Reference the specific
    thing they wrote or did, in natural sentence case. A marketing-flavored
    subject gets scanned and deleted. [02] (See the house subject rules in
    `playbooks/_shared.md`.)

## Common failure patterns

- Seller-centric opener ("I have a product relevant to you / we help teams
  like…") that talks about the sender. [05]
- Assumptive "you need it" framing that tells the buyer their own state. [05]
- Feature-dump or product-name opener before any problem or alternative is
  framed. [10]
- Floating statistics with no cause; a quote with its attribution trailing
  after it. [05]
- Buzzword pileup and "AI-powered platform" openers. [06][10]
- A time/meeting-request CTA, or a long open-ended question, in a cold first
  touch. [06]
- Multiple asks or a research-heavy ask that makes the reader do work. [06][04]
- A wall of text on mobile despite a low word count. [05]
- FOMO / "falling behind" pressure. [10]

## Bad / good example pairs

- **Bad:** "I have a product relevant to your work on the sales team."
  **Good:** "What are you using for e-signatures right now?"
  **Why:** cuts the seller-centric opener; a question frames context and pulls
  the reader in without telling them what they need. [05]

- **Bad:** "Our AI-powered platform optimizes and streamlines your estimating
  workflow."
  **Good:** "Contractors tell us losing a bid to four other roofers after all
  that estimating stings — a couple stopped losing those deals by changing one
  step."
  **Why:** buzzwords and "AI" depress replies; the buyer is sold on their own
  priority, not your mechanism. [06][10]

- **Bad:** "Are you free for 15 minutes this Friday at 2pm?"
  **Good:** "Want me to send the two-line version of what those teams changed?"
  **Why:** meeting/time asks were the worst-performing CTA; an offer-based ask is
  low-effort and gives value for time. [06]

- **Bad:** "We're [Product]. We do assignments, prioritization, shared inbox, and
  analytics — can I show you a demo?"
  **Good:** "Most teams your size start on a shared inbox, then hit a wall when
  they need real assignment and prioritization. Is that roughly where you are, or
  past it?"
  **Why:** leads with the buyer's situation and real alternative, asks one
  confirmable question, and names no features until value is established. [10]

- **Bad:** "They saved 10+ hours a week and grew revenue 50%." (bare stats)
  **Good:** "They were losing time and money to manual doc creation — 10+ hours a
  week — until that step went away."
  **Why:** the numbers stick when attached to the cause. [05]

## Conditions under which these rules apply

- The length, mobile-formatting, and single-ask rules bind on **cold, skim-read
  first touches**; they may relax on a warm/replied thread or when the recipient
  has asked for detail. [06]
- The **no open-ended question** caution is a cold-first-touch finding; open
  questions are fine on calls and in later discovery. [06]
- The **offer-based CTA** presumes the offer is genuinely and quickly deliverable
  — promise only an audit/benchmark/sample the team can actually produce. Never a
  bait offer. [06]
- **Personalization priority branches on seniority:** for director+ contacts,
  activity- and company-level anchors work; for individual contributors and
  managers, individual-level personalization is preferred. [06]
- **Name the offer late** when the buyer likely hasn't bought the category before
  or several alternatives compete; you may state it earlier only when the sole
  realistic alternative is a plainly bad status quo. [10]
- The full positioning arc (insight → alternatives → perfect world → offer →
  proof → objections → ask) is a **multi-touch sequence**, not one email;
  compressing it all into one cold email is itself a failure. [10]

## Evaluation checklist

1. Does the first sentence talk about the recipient (a question or observed
   event), not the sender or the product? → else fail.
2. Is the email roughly 50–100 words, 3–4 sentences, in short mobile-readable
   blocks? → else flag.
3. Is there exactly one ask / one CTA? → else fail.
4. Is the CTA offer- or interest-based, not a meeting/time request or a long
   open-ended question? → else fail.
5. Is the email free of banned buzzwords and of an "AI/solution-first" opener?
   → else fail.
6. For every capability named, is the buyer-facing value ("so what?") stated, and
   is at least one real alternative referenced before the offer? → else flag.
7. Is every statistic attached to a cause, and every quote given context before
   it? → else flag.
8. Is FOMO / "falling behind" pressure absent? → else fail.
9. Read aloud: does each sentence sound like something Andrew would say, and drive
   the next? → else revise.
