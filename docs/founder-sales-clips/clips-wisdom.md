going to market. This is the source registry behind the managed

## Evidence boundary

These are self-reported founder and creator anecdotes, several from consumer,
creator-tool, or high-volume motions that differ from Andrew's evidence-led,
human-approved, low-volume B2B outreach. Treat every claim as practitioner
signal to test, never as proof. Revenue, valuation, reply-rate, and
conversion-rate figures ([C1] "1,000 sent, 10-20 reply"; [C3] "50-70% waitlist
opt-in", "50% free-to-paid", "$3M ARR / $85M / 60-day $1M ARR") are promotional
and unverified. Do not put them in outbound copy or use them as forecasts. The
handbook's evidence, role-fit, clarity, single-CTA, no-link, deliverability, and
suppression gates win on every conflict.

## What improves the emails (adopted, bounded)

### 1. Diagnose, don't demo — lead with a specific finding, not an ask [C3]

The single most on-point line across the three clips. [C3]: *"Instead of leading
with 'Hey, would you like a demo?', offer to diagnose something for them."* His
example opener: *"I've been looking at your business, and I think there are three
clear opportunities around the problem that you solve... I'm happy to jump on a
call and talk you through what I'm seeing."* This starts a conversation, adds
value, and earns the call.

- This reinforces the handbook's existing diagnostic-offer pattern, GnK's
  "bring a build hypothesis," and OutageHub's "bring a real record." Keep it.
- **Bounded by evidence discipline:** the "three opportunities" must be
  source-grounded observations or clearly-labelled hypotheses to correct, never
  invented internal facts. A diagnosis is a proposed example the recipient can
  correct, not a claim about what is broken inside their company.
- **Conflict with the no-link rule:** [C3] drops the diagnosis "into a document"
  and personalizes outbound with enrichment tools (Apollo, LinkedIn, Clay). The
  handbook forbids links, attachments, and tracking in cold email. Put the
  finding *in the email body*, or offer to share it on the call. Do not attach or
  link a doc in a first touch.

### 2. Frame value in the recipient's terms, concretely [C1]

[C1]: *"It doesn't matter what I think the value is. The only thing that matters
is the value in the eyes of the person you want to use your product."* He led not
with features but with the one thing that annoyed sellers (resetting keys at 10pm
on a Friday, chasing Venmo requests), made vivid with a plain analogy (*"imagine
every time you rented your Netflix account you had to reset the password"*).

- Reinforces "write around the prospect's problem" and the plain-English rule.
- **Adopted technique:** one honest, everyday analogy can make an operational
  cost land faster than jargon. The analogy must be truthful and must not imply a
  measured claim. It supports the existing "explain the idea before naming it"
  rule.

### 3. Sound like a person talking, not an email [C1]

[C1]: read outreach out loud; *"if it sounds weird, don't send."* Draft by
imagining you met the person at a bar and have 20 seconds; record a voice memo,
then write from how you actually said it. Nobody says *"I'm reaching out on
behalf of a platform called X"* to a human, yet people write it constantly.

- This is a concrete method for the handbook's existing "read every line aloud"
  rule. Adopted, and `reaching out on behalf of` added to the banned openers.

### 4. Reduce decision load — give one clear recommended next step [C2]

[C2]: when people don't buy, it's often because there is no clear A → B → C, so
they default to *"just send it over, I'll think about it"* — a soft no. A clear
*"this is the problem, this is the solution, this is the plan"* earns *"okay,
sounds good."* People want the lead taken and an easy yes.

- Reinforces the single-CTA rule, the answerability test, and Gate 3 (a complete
  thought). The fix for a stalled email is usually *more clarity and one obvious
  next step*, not more options or a softer, vaguer ask.
- **Bounded hard:** "easy yes" here means clarity, not pressure. It does **not**
  license presumptive scheduling, false urgency, "take the lead" pushiness, or
  removing the recipient's easy out. Honest routing ("do you own this, or who
  does?") remains a valid win. One clear next step, calmly offered.

### 5. Stand out with real, one-to-one effort — never generic automation [C1]

[C1]: *"Everyone sends cold emails... you have to do something different,"* and
generic automated messages *"do not work at all."* Memories are sparked by things
that surprise people; visible effort signals care.

- The transferable core is already handbook doctrine: every message is unique, no
  templates, no reused structure, personalization tied to a real reason.
- **Rejected tactics (out of bounds):** [C1]'s attention-getters — sending an
  Uber driver to a prospect's house, edible arrangements, texting a personal
  iCloud email, or a selfie video claiming *"the engineers won't leave the office
  until you reply"* — violate the handbook's rules against fabrication, false
  urgency, invasive contact, and deliverability discipline. Do not use them.
  Effort should show as specific, genuine relevance, not stunts.

## Targeting & GTM context (useful, mostly outside the writer's brain)

These shape research and campaign strategy (§2 of the handbook) more than email
copy. Recorded here; not promoted into the writer block.

- **Insert into a conversation already happening [C3].** Validate demand by
  finding a large group already complaining about the same problem, then join
  that conversation. Matches the handbook's "why now / real trigger" discipline:
  a live, observable conversation is a stronger trigger than a title. [C3]
- **Existing traditional-method spend validates budget [C3].** "No competitors"
  is a red flag; prior tools solving the problem the old way prove budget exists.
  Compatible with the budget-signal discipline in §2. [C3]
- **Be specific about who and where [C1].** Not "product designers" but "a
  product designer in New York who follows Figma"; go where they already are.
  Reinforces narrow-ICP + reachable-contact discipline. [C1]
- **Land small-to-big within a market [C1].** Get smaller, accessible players
  using it and referring, then pursue the anchor account. Sequencing insight for
  campaign planning, not a copy rule. [C1]
- **Free / low-friction wedge, then expand [C1][C3].** [C1] kept the seller side
  free for 18 months ("never give someone a reason not to use it"); [C3] used a
  free AI-visibility report as the lead magnet. Mirrors OutageHub's free-map
  wedge. A GTM/offer choice, not an email rule; note the no-link constraint still
  applies to how any "free thing" is offered in cold email. [C1][C3]
- **Build the audience before the product [C3].** Waitlist + educational,
  value-first content so launch meets existing intent. Relevant to Andrew's own
  founder-led presence, not to the cold sequence. [C3]
- **Watch users in silence for feedback [C1].** On discovery calls, give one
  goal, go on mute, watch where they get stuck, then ask "what was most confusing
  / why did you pause here." Supports the discovery-call posture, not email copy. [C1]

## Conflict log (what these clips get wrong for this project)

- **Volume and automation [C1][C3].** Both lean on scale ([C1] "send to 1,000",
  [C3] enrichment-driven outbound at volume). This project is deliberately
  low-volume, human-approved, and deliverability-safe. Keep the *relevance* and
  *value-first* lessons; reject the volume framing.
- **Links, docs, and lead magnets in the ask [C3].** Compatible in spirit, but
  the handbook's no-link / no-attachment first-touch rule stands. Deliver the
  value in words; offer the artifact for the call.
- **"Take the lead / tell them what to do" [C2].** Adopt as *clarity and one
  clear next step*; never as pressure, presumptive booking, or removing the out.
- **Reported conversion and revenue numbers [C1][C3].** Unverified and
  promotional. Never quote them, never use them as targets.
