# Shared outreach rules

This file governs every campaign. Campaign files add offer and audience context
but cannot relax the evidence, role-fit, clarity, or quality gates below.

## The goal

Earn a thoughtful reply from a well-chosen person. Do not optimize for the
shortest possible email, a clever subject, fake urgency, or an immediate sale.
The reader should understand why Andrew chose them, what he is working on, why a
conversation could be useful, and the one question he wants them to answer.

Length is not the goal, but thin emails do not earn time. Touch 1 should usually
contain 90 to 145 body words, excluding greeting and signature. Use 4 to 6
natural sentences across 3 short paragraphs. Later emails can be shorter when
they add one real idea. Never add filler merely to hit a word count.

## Default seven-touch sequence

This is the default for campaigns without an explicit override. A campaign file
may use a different cadence and set of touch jobs while preserving every shared
evidence, role-fit, clarity, suppression, and quality gate. Wapahki defines a
campaign-specific seven-touch sequence; OutageHub defines a four-email sequence.

| Touch | Day | Channel | Job |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | email | Best evidence-led email. Earn a 20-minute conversation or a useful reply. |
| 2 | 4 | email | Same thread. Add one useful detail, example, or easier way to answer. |
| 3 | 6 | LinkedIn connect | Under 200 characters. A human reason to connect, no meeting request. |
| 4 | 9 | email | New thread and new subject. A genuinely different, role-relevant angle. |
| 5 | 11 | LinkedIn message | One concrete question in the recipient's remit. |
| 6 | 15 | email | Substantive note. Clarify the value and offer a useful artifact. |
| 7 | 18 | email | Short closing email. Make it easy to route or decline; no guilt or pressure. |

Email touches have a greeting, blank lines between paragraphs, and this signature
(Andrew's full name, then the campaign signature line):

```text
Thanks,
Andrew Gordienko
{Campaign signature line}
```

LinkedIn messages have no email signature.

## The narrative arc: what we want and how each touch earns it

The configured touches are one story, not repeated asks. Every touch does one
job that moves the same relationship forward. What we want is always the same:

> **Earn a 20-minute discovery call with the right operator** by proving we
> understand their floor and have something specific to learn from them — then
> convert that call into the real commitment.

The real commitment, by business (name it in the private brief's next_step):

- **Wapahki:** a 20-minute learning call or a useful email answer. Any future
  product or commercial path must be earned outside the cold sequence after the
  problem and process are understood.
- **GnK:** a 20-minute call, then a scoped **30/60/90 engagement** — a decision
  system proven in 30 to 90 days.
- **OutageHub:** a 20-minute decision-validation call. A historical validation
  or first-year deployment is proposed only after discovery establishes the
  workflow, evidence bar, integration, and measurable outcome.

Each touch and what it is trying to get:

| Touch | Channel | Its one job | What we want from them |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | email | **Open the problem.** One true, specific fact about their operation plus one diagnostic question about which difficulty applies. | A reply, or the 20-minute call. The signal we earned: "they understand my floor." |
| 2 | email (same thread) | **Add value.** Bring one concrete thing they did not have, such as a rough cost model or a real record. Lower the bar to answer. | A one-line reply. Proof we are worth 20 minutes. |
| 3 | LinkedIn connect (≤200) | **Become familiar.** One human sentence naming the shared topic, no ask. | The connection accepted, so we are a known name and not a cold email. |
| 4 | email (new angle) | **New angle.** A different role-relevant source, lesson, or reframed consequence. | Re-engage a different nerve: a reply or the call. |
| 5 | LinkedIn DM | **Role question.** Now connected, one direct, easy, specific question in their remit. | An easy one-line answer that starts a real conversation. |
| 6 | email | **Offer the artifact.** State the offer at real maturity and the specific payoff of a call; offer a useful artifact (a one-page sketch, a sample record). | The meeting, or a yes to the artifact. |
| 7 | email | **Close the loop.** Make it easy to route to the right owner or to decline, no guilt, door left open. | A routing answer or a final yes; otherwise a clean exit that keeps the door open. |

Discipline that keeps the arc honest: confidence never grows past the evidence,
no touch repeats another's question or CTA, only touch 1 (and sparingly touch 6)
asks for the call, and a touch with nothing genuinely new is not sent. If the
right person is uncertain, a routing question ("do you own this, or who does?")
is a valid win at any touch.

## When to send (by industry)

Timing decides whether a message is seen, not whether it persuades. Targeting,
trigger, and relevance are the larger levers and come first. The app does not
send, so this is guidance for the person doing the manual send. Treat every time
below as a testable default measured by reply quality, not opens.

Core rules, all campaigns:

- Send on the recipient's working clock, not a generic 9am. A plant supervisor's
  morning starts near 5 to 6am; a claims consultant's near 8am. Aim for the first
  time that role realistically opens an inbox.
- Default to Tuesday through Thursday. Avoid Monday morning (weekend backlog) and
  Friday afternoon (checked out). A default to test, not a law.
- Align to the prospect's timezone. Accounts here are mostly Ontario and Canada
  (Eastern), with UK football clubs on GMT or BST.
- Spread sends across the window and across days. Blasting every message at one
  "ideal" minute trips spam filters and drops you into the same select-all-delete
  pile as every other early sender.
- The Sunday about 10am or Monday about 7am schedule-send helps week-preppers
  (executives, directors, plant and ops managers) whose first task is planning the
  week. It does little for shift or operator roles who do not run their week from
  email.

By business and role:

- Wapahki (plants, warehouses). Shift-based, early-start roles: production and
  plant managers, maintenance, warehouse supervisors, operators, process and
  automation engineers. Best window 6:30 to 8:00am local, before the production
  day ramps; warehouse and logistics roles closer to 6am. Second best is right
  after a shift ends or the early-afternoon lull around 1 to 2pm. Avoid mid-
  morning through mid-afternoon, when they are on the floor, and anything after
  4pm. These buyers live in email less than desk workers, so an early note read on
  a phone beats a mid-day desktop send.

- GnK, delay (construction and claims). Project directors, commercial and claims
  leads, claims consultants, schedulers. Sites start early, but the buyers are
  office or hybrid: 7:00 to 8:30am before meetings, or the Sunday-evening and
  early-Monday planning window for directors. Claims consultants are professional
  services, so Tuesday through Thursday mornings. Construction PMs often walk site
  Monday morning, so avoid Monday sends to them.

- GnK, football (opposition analysis). Sporting directors, heads of analysis,
  recruitment and performance. Their week revolves around fixtures. Avoid
  matchdays and the day after (a Saturday match means avoid Friday afternoon,
  Saturday, and Sunday; a midweek fixture shifts this). Analysts are at desks
  Tuesday through Thursday mornings. A transfer window is a relevance trigger, not
  a send time.

- GnK, general software and AI (tech and ops orgs). Standard knowledge workers:
  Tuesday through Thursday, 7 to 9am local for top-of-inbox, or the early-afternoon
  check around 1 to 2pm. Sunday late-afternoon or Monday 7am schedule-send for
  executives.

- OutageHub (utility, facility, telecom, EV, field service, municipal ops).
  Operations managers, dispatch and on-call, emergency power. Early-start ops:
  6:30 to 8:00am, midweek. Some run around the clock, but a cold email during an
  active outage is ignored because they are firefighting, so send in steady state.
  For this audience the real outage record in touch 2 matters far more than the slot.

Any timing claim is a hypothesis. When testing, hold the audience, offer, and
touch job constant, change one timing variable at a time, and compare reply
quality by segment. Do not import a time that worked for one industry into
another. Source: r/sales practitioner thread (segment-and-test, warehouse 6am,
Sunday schedule-send, spread-to-avoid-spam) plus role-schedule reasoning.

## Gate 1: evidence

**The governing hard rule.** Public evidence may establish the company's
environment, but it cannot establish the private workflow, its owner, its
difficulty or its financial consequence. Four things are private and stay a
hypothesis until the recipient confirms them:

1. **The workflow** — how they actually do the thing today.
2. **The owner** — who performs it and who is accountable for it. A title never
   proves ownership; ask or route, do not infer.
3. **The difficulty** — where it is hard, slow, manual, or error-prone.
4. **The financial consequence** — what it costs in money, hours, or risk. Do not
   state or quantify it in a cold email; at most point at a metric the recipient
   can confirm.

Full detail and the review checklists are in `docs/outbound-doctrine/`
(handbooks A and B).

The contact context separates `verified_evidence` from
`unverified_research_hypotheses`. That distinction is absolute.

- A company-specific factual claim must be supported by a source in
  `verified_evidence`.
- A person's title and the public description of the company are facts. A title
  does not prove a pain, workflow, priority, or buying authority.
- `relevance_reason`, `defensible_problem`, `ai_project`, and researcher-written
  angles are hypotheses. They may inspire a question or proposed example, but
  must never be written as something known about the recipient.
- A peer example is context, not FOMO. Explain the practical connection. Never
  imply the recipient is behind, should copy the peer, or can beat a larger
  organization merely because they are smaller.
- Do not turn a source into a stronger claim than it supports. A palletizer that
  accepts box dimensions does not prove a bakery switches bread formats. A
  historical case about a league's launch does not prove its current internal
  workflow.
- No invented client, metric, installation, outage lead time, cost saving,
  internal process, or product capability.

If the evidence is too thin to earn an email, return `do_not_contact`. A missing
draft is better than polished nonsense.

## Gate 2: role fit

Before writing, state privately in one sentence why this exact role would care
and what they can credibly answer or decide.

- Write to the person's remit, not a generic company problem.
- Floor staff can explain the work. Do not ask them to buy or approve a pilot.
- Maintenance can discuss recovery, access, training, and support. Do not assume
  they wait on vendors.
- Technical leaders can assess feasibility and ownership.
- Operations leaders can discuss response and operating workflow.
- A marketing or partnerships title is not automatically a buyer for an
  operations or engineering product. If no honest route exists, do not draft.
- Do not mention the recipient's title in the email as a mail-merge device.

## Gate 3: a complete thought

Touch 1 needs four ingredients, in a natural order:

1. A verified reason for writing now.
2. One open question the recipient is qualified to answer.
3. A plain explanation of what Andrew is building or can deliver.
4. One call to action with a reason the conversation is worth their time.

This is not a rigid template. Vary the sentence shape and order, but do not omit
the logic. The offer must connect directly to the question.

Apply an answerability test before drafting: could the recipient answer the
main question in one ordinary sentence without first learning Andrew's product
vocabulary or deciding what an abstract phrase means? If not, rewrite it.
Questions about one visible event pass, such as changing to a different box
size or seeing an error message after a robot stops. Questions about what
someone should `be able to do`, what `would need to be true`, or two events
joined with `or` fail.

When asking for a call, ask for 20 minutes. Never ask for 10 or 15. Do not call a
20-minute request "quick." A first touch may ask for 20 minutes when the email
has earned it. Other touches can offer a one-page idea, sample record, or
specific question instead.

For a first-touch call CTA, use two sentences. The first asks plainly for a
20-minute call, and may name a loose window such as "next week". The second
either says what Andrew will bring and what the recipient can help correct, or
offers a lower-friction alternative such as "An email reply would also be really
helpful." Do not cram the request, condition, artifact, recipient's job, and
outcome into one sentence with `if` and `so`.

Use one CTA. An easy out is optional and should sound calm, not needy. Do not
repeat "a no is helpful," "tell me to get lost," or similar rejection requests.
Do not command the recipient to disprove Andrew's premise with phrases such as
`tell me if that is not a problem`. Do not end with a dismissal such as `no
need to continue`. The writer is responsible for qualification.

## Native, plain English

Write as Andrew speaking to a capable colleague. Read every line aloud.

- Translate the offer before writing the email. Use a concrete actor, action,
  and object. Who does what to which thing? If that cannot be said in one plain
  sentence, the idea is not ready for outreach.
- Explain a physical or technical idea before naming it. `Flexible equipment`
  means plant staff can set the robot up for a different product or box without
  asking an integrator to rewrite the whole program. A `robot cell` means the
  robot system doing the packing or handling job. `Re-teach a format` means set
  the robot up for the next product or box. Use the explanation, not the label.
- The same rule applies to software. A `scenario sandbox` is a small internal
  tool that runs one proposed rule against sample cases before the live system
  changes. An `outage feed` is a record of which area a utility reported as out
  and when. Say the concrete meaning first.
- Prefer concrete subjects and verbs.
- Use contractions.
- Keep one idea per sentence.
- Keep one operational thread per first email. The evidence, question,
  explanation of the offer, and call payoff must all concern the same situation.
  Do not start with a stopped machine, switch to changing products, and end with
  integrator programming. Those may be related product goals, but they are
  different conversations.
- Make every pronoun easy to resolve. If `it`, `that`, or `this` could refer to
  the robot, the screen, the program, or the process, repeat the noun.
- Use the words the recipient would use at work, but avoid jargon Andrew cannot
  support.
- One modest uncertainty marker is enough. Repeated hedging makes the offer
  sound unformed.
- Do not hide the premise inside a long hedge such as `while I don't know
  whether your company uses robots, I'd value your view as I design what should
  happen after one stops`. Split the ideas, introduce the noun before using a
  pronoun, and ask the role-relevant question directly.
- Never use abstract consultant English such as "what would need to be true
  before you'd trust that," "what would maintenance challenge first," or
  "take this off your plate" when the recipient never put anything on a plate.
- Avoid canned filler such as "quick one," "genuine question," "different
  question this time," "the reason I keep at this," and "no pressure at all."
- Avoid phrases that make the recipient administer the outreach, including
  "tell me if that's not a problem," "if it doesn't map to your work," and "no
  need to continue."
- Do not swap awkward English for boardroom English. Andrew should not say
  `strategically useful`, `assess whether the idea merits`, `deserves internal
  attention`, `outside current priorities`, `if applicable`, or `at this stage`.
  Say `would that help`, `is this useful`, `is this something you are working
  on`, or name the decision directly.
- `Caught my attention because` is a model transition, not a human reason for
  writing. State the fact, then say what it made Andrew want to ask.
- `That made me want to ask`, `that work made me curious`, and `seeing that
  range` are also model transitions when they merely bridge two paragraphs.
  State the public fact, then ask the concrete question.
- Avoid dense noun piles such as `operator steps`, `format-change workflow`,
  `palletizing choices`, `decision inputs`, and `scenario acceptance checks`.
  Turn them into people doing things.
- Do not ask the recipient to `correct my view`, `correct the checks`, or
  `correct what the person decides`. Say what Andrew will show, then ask whether
  it matches the recipient's work or what Andrew has overlooked.
- Avoid `you can correct...` in the CTA. It sounds like Andrew has assigned the
  reader editing work. Prefer `you can tell me what I missed`, `you can compare
  it with the way your team works`, or one equally specific natural sentence.
- Do not ask whether one record `belongs beside` another. Say what the person
  would compare and the decision that comparison could change.
- Do not repeat the same long phrase in adjacent paragraphs. Repetition is not
  clarity. State the situation once, then move the idea forward.
- Do not join two different events with `or` to make a question sound broad.
  `After a routine stop or pack change` is not one situation. Pick the stop or
  the new box and ask one answerable question.
- A sentence can be grammatical and still fail. Reject it if a native speaker
  would pause to decode what the noun means, who is acting, or why one clause
  follows the next.

Hard bans:

- Em dashes and en dashes
- Exclamation points
- Colons in subjects or body copy
- Bullets or numbered lists inside buyer-facing messages
- Buzzwords such as leverage, unlock, seamless, revolutionary, cutting-edge,
  synergy, and streamline
- "Hope this finds you well," "I wanted to reach out," "just following up,"
  "just checking in," "I found you because," and "my guess is"
- "Reaching out on behalf of," and any phrasing you would never say to the
  person out loud. Read the line aloud, or say it as a 20-second voice memo
  first. If it sounds like an email and not like Andrew talking, rewrite it.
- Links, tracking language, or calendar links in cold email
- Pay-on-delivery language such as "only pay if it ships" or "you don't pay if
  it doesn't work"

## Subjects

Use 2 to 5 words with natural sentence capitalization: capitalize the first
word and genuine proper nouns or acronyms, not every word. Make the subject
specific enough to be credible and plain enough to look human.

Good subjects describe the real topic, question, or evidence:

- `Bakery palletizing`
- `Who resets the cell`
- `Weekly scoring tests`
- `July storm timing`

Avoid:

- vague curiosity bait such as `Before the big leagues`
- accusatory or ambulance-chasing subjects such as `Your July outage`
- unsupported loss claims such as `Always last to know`
- generic nouns such as `Update`, `Timing`, or `Automation`
- fake internal-thread subjects or question marks

Review subjects as a campaign-plan set, not as isolated decoration. T2 keeps
T1's subject exactly when it is a reply in the same thread. Each later email
thread earns a new subject that reflects its distinct job, not a cosmetic
singular/plural or synonym rewrite.

For Wapahki's seven-touch sequence, generate independent subjects for T1, T4,
and T7. T2 retains T1 exactly, and T5 retains T4 exactly. T1 names the concrete
role-specific question, T4 names the emerging hypothesis, and T7 closes that
specific learning thread. All three independent subjects must be materially
distinct.

For OutageHub's four-email sequence, generate independent subjects for T1, T2,
and T4. T2 opens the replay thread, T3 retains T2 exactly, and T4 closes or
routes ownership. The three independent subjects must be materially distinct.

For every independent email thread, the subject strategist must produce six
evidence-grounded candidates. A skeptical critic challenges truthfulness,
recipient fit, exact-message alignment, natural inbox language, and
distinctiveness before an independent editor chooses or rewrites the final
subject. A missing, duplicated, ungrounded, generically salesy, wrongly
capitalized, or semantically recycled subject fails closed. Do not store any
subject set unless the whole set passes; copy an approved subject only to
touches that explicitly share its thread.

## Review panel and fail-closed rule

Every generated sequence goes through four named editorial lenses:

1. Evidence editor. Identify claims the supplied sources do not support.
2. Recipient editor. Check role fit and whether the question belongs in this
   person's remit.
3. Clarity editor. Remove awkward or non-native English and make the message
   understandable on one read.
4. Reply editor. Check that the message gives enough context and a concrete
   reason to answer.

The panel may return `pass`, `revise`, or `do_not_contact`. A failed or missing
review is not approval. The pipeline must store nothing unless the reviewed
version passes deterministic formatting and language checks.

The clarity editor also runs as a separate, independent read-aloud pass. It must
quote each line that is grammatical but unnatural, explain why a person would
stumble, and replace it with spoken English. "Technically correct" is a revise.
It must also trace the logic across paragraphs. A strong opener followed by a
muddled product explanation is a revise even when each sentence works alone.

## Deal-specific rehearsal before writing

The private preparation should be richer than the buyer-facing message. Before
drafting, complete the normal spoken brief and add three checks:

1. **Skeptical question.** Name the hardest credible question this role is
   likely to ask about fit, proof, implementation, risk, or ownership. Rehearse
   the answer before sending. Do not stuff the answer into touch 1 unless it is
   necessary to understand the offer.
2. **Proof boundary.** State exactly what the supplied evidence, real product,
   or authorized customer result supports and what it does not. A case from
   another company does not prove the same result here. If relevant proof is
   missing, offer a sketch, sample record, fixed-fee pilot, or bounded test.
3. **Next step.** Decide the one concrete action that should follow if the
   recipient confirms relevance. It may be a role-specific 20-minute
   conversation, a review of one artifact, a technical check, a pilot-scoping
   step, or a routing introduction. Do not ask for several paths at once.

Use public personal context only when it is genuine, relevant, and natural.
Never force a hobby, school, sports team, or mutual interest into the opener as
decoration. Business specificity and a credible reason to reply come first.

After a real conversation, capture the buyer's wording, objection, proof request,
decision, and dated next action. Promote a lesson into this playbook only after
it repeats or materially changes how the team should qualify, explain, or
advance an opportunity.

## Sequence-level review

Read every touch in the active campaign plan together.

- Do not repeat the same question, CTA, apology, or sentence.
- Follow-ups must add information rather than announce that they are follow-ups.
- A second angle must still fit the role.
- Do not make every touch ask for 20 minutes.
- Do not become more confident than the evidence over time.
- End calmly. No guilt, artificial scarcity, or request for a rejection.

## Reddit practitioner refinements

Add these as tests and safeguards, not universal laws.

### Fit and timing

- Before drafting, classify the reason for outreach as a real trigger, observable issue, routing question, or weak hypothesis. If it is only title plus industry, treat it as insufficient unless the campaign file supplies a defensible current trigger. [R003] [R008] [R014] [R016]
- Prefer triggers tied to present business change or visible workflow strain, such as hiring, leadership change, vendor evaluation, public posts, events, funding or budget changes, operational disruption, tool switching, or a concrete site/workflow issue. Do not imply the trigger proves pain unless the source supports that. [R003] [R005] [R008] [R009] [R014] [R020] [R023]
- Fast disqualification is a valid outcome. A draft may ask whether the problem is owned, relevant, or correctly routed instead of trying to sell the full meeting in every touch. [R002] [R003] [R004] [R013]

### CTA choices

- When evidence is specific but the relationship is cold, consider a lower-friction CTA before a meeting ask: permission to send the short version, asking whether the issue is on their plate, or asking who owns the problem. Test this against the 20-minute CTA by sequence stage, not as a blanket replacement. [R005] [R009] [R012] [R014] [R015]
- If the stakeholder is uncertain, do not fake certainty. Use a right-person CTA that explains the business reason and asks whether they own it or whether someone else does. [R007] [R009] [R014] [R015]
- Calendar links and direct booking asks are disputed. Keep the existing no-calendar-link rule for first-touch cold email, and test meeting asks only after the message has earned enough relevance. [R005] [R009] [R014] [R015]

### Follow-up logic

- Each follow-up should do one new job: add a verified detail, reframe the same evidence for a different role-relevant consequence, offer a useful artifact, answer a likely objection, or clarify routing. Do not merely increase pressure or restate the first ask. [R004] [R012] [R014] [R022] [R023]
- Treat fixed touch-count advice as a campaign hypothesis. The configured campaign sequence is an operating design, not a universal law; review negative replies, unsubscribe signals, low-fit responses, and downstream quality before increasing persistence. [R005] [R006] [R010] [R012] [R014]

### Deliverability-safe copy

- First-touch emails should stay plain text and avoid attachments, heavy formatting, images, unsolicited links, deceptive subjects, fake replies, fake forwards, and tracking language. This is copy hygiene as well as deliverability hygiene. [R005] [R010] [R014] [R015]
- Do not use deliverability tactics to evade recipient preferences or spam controls. If a campaign depends on domain hopping, provider churn, broad scraping, or burned-domain logic, return `do_not_contact` or require human review. [R006] [R010]
- Bad data is a writing problem too. If the contact record suggests low-quality, invalid, scraped, or poorly matched data, do not compensate with vaguer copy. Flag the list quality issue. [R012] [R015]

### What to test

- Test ICP and trigger quality before testing subject lines or small wording changes. Weak timing can make good copy look bad, and strong timing can make imperfect copy work. [R003] [R004] [R014] [R016]
- Test problem-led copy against service-led copy using the same ICP and similar trigger quality. Do not compare across unrelated markets or buyer motions. [R008] [R012] [R016]
- Segment results by ICP, channel, offer, buyer maturity, ACV, and sales motion. Do not import lessons from territory sales, freelancer outreach, creator communities, enterprise sales, or SaaS email without context. [R001] [R005] [R006] [R016] [R022]
- Measure replies, qualified conversations, meetings, pipeline quality, clients closed, retention fit, list quality, negative replies, and downstream quality. Opens, sends, and last-touch attribution are diagnostic signals, not proof of persuasion. [R005] [R006] [R010] [R012] [R014] [R022]

## Active YouTube, course, and book refinements

These are the compatible writing rules distilled from the cited cross-source
research in `docs/sales-research/research-wisdom.md`. The handbook and campaign
rules still win. In particular, the configured campaign touch plan, a 20-minute
scope whenever a call is requested, the first-touch completeness range, and the
no-link rule remain this project's house standards.

### Research and timing

- Build a falsifiable account hypothesis before writing. Keep the observed fact,
  its source, one possible role-relevant implication, what is still unknown, and
  the next fact the conversation should test. [S001] [S004] [S005] [S006] [S019]
  [S028]
- Treat hiring, news, funding, reviews, technology use, and peer activity as
  reasons to ask a better question, not proof of pain, budget, authority, or
  urgency. Connect one verified signal to one plausible implication. Do not pile
  up signals. [S004] [S010] [S028] [S040] [S046]
- Prefer current primary company material and buyer-described facts. An old case
  can explain company background but cannot create a current problem by itself.
  [S001] [S005] [S006] [S019]
- In an early market, honest research is a valid posture. Ask a bounded question
  that tests the problem or the proposed mechanism. Do not hide missing product
  clarity behind a sales claim. [S007] [S019] [S020] [S026]

### Contact and buying route

- Identify whether this person is a user, evaluator, champion, budget owner,
  implementation stakeholder, or possible router. Seniority alone does not
  establish the route. [S010] [S027] [S029] [S031]
- A routing reply is useful. When ownership is genuinely uncertain but the
  business reason is strong, ask whether they own the question or who does.
  Never use a vague `outside your remit` escape line. [S010] [S034] [S035]

### Copy and CTA

- Use one truthful context, one possible implication, one concrete mechanism or
  artifact, and one next step. This is reasoning order, not a mail-merge
  template. [S006] [S010] [S011] [S019]
- Scope proof exactly. A comparable example can show what happened elsewhere;
  it cannot promise the same result here. Where no proof exists, offer a sketch,
  sample record, or tightly bounded question instead of inventing a result.
  [S002] [S006] [S007] [S030]
- Choose one CTA based on evidence strength. A strong role and current trigger
  may earn a direct 20-minute call. Thinner but still credible evidence may earn
  permission to send a useful artifact or a routing question. Never combine all
  three. [S001] [S002] [S003] [S004] [S007] [S020] [S046]
- Each follow-up must add a verified detail, useful artifact, new role-relevant
  question, or routing clarification. More pressure is not new value. [S003]
  [S004] [S010] [S012] [S046]

### Human judgment

- AI can organize evidence, draft constrained variants, and stress-test an
  angle. It cannot turn a hypothesis into an account fact or make the final
  judgment on role fit, tone, proof, privacy, promises, or whether an email
  should be sent. [S002] [S012] [S019] [S020] [S040] [S042]
- Judge experiments by qualified conversations and downstream fit, not opens,
  raw replies, or send volume. Test targeting and problem strength before small
  wording changes. [S002] [S007] [S014] [S020] [S032] [S046]

## Promoted cross-source refinements

The full evidence and conflict log lives in
`docs/sales-research/research-wisdom.md`. These compatible refinements are
operational:

- Treat third-party intent, enrichment, and hiring data as research leads, not
  message-ready evidence. Verify a message-critical signal against a company
  source or another credible primary source.
- Scope proof honestly. A customer name requires authorization; a comparison
  must be relevant in industry, scale, geography, or operating context; and the
  message must name the correct outcome category. Pipeline, revenue, profit,
  reliability, and workload are not interchangeable.
- Choose the CTA from the evidence. An evidenced urgent problem can earn a
  specific 20-minute agenda. Plausible but unproven relevance usually earns an
  offer to share a useful artifact or an interest check. Uncertain ownership
  earns a routing question. Early-market learning earns a relevance question.
  Every message still has exactly one request.
- LinkedIn must add familiarity or distinct role-relevant context. Never paste
  the email pitch into a connection request or DM. SMS requires explicit
  permission and is outside this sequence.

## Endorsed touch-1 pattern

These are distilled from real first-touch emails Andrew refined to "almost perfect"
across GnK and Wapahki. Every campaign's touch 1 should follow this shape, tuned to
the product and the recipient's role. It works because it grounds on a public fact,
names a specific difficulty as a calibrated hypothesis, shows the human cost, and asks the
recipient to correct it.

Order:

1. **Start with their problem, not Andrew's prospecting process.** Use one
   concrete, true fact about what they or the company does to make the problem
   specific. Never write "I found you because".
2. **A specific diagnostic question about their workflow.** Either "how much manual
   work is involved in [specific task]?" or a multiple-choice "where does the most
   [X] time go — is it A, B, or C?" It must be answerable in one sentence and prove
   you understand the process.
3. **Calibrate the hypothesis.** Use "I suspect the difficult part is [specific
   difficulty]" or ask whether that part is difficult. Never write "My guess is"
   or assert that the recipient's process breaks.
4. **The human / operational cost, concretely** — "families waiting weeks", "more
   follow-up for staff", "pushes work back onto operators". A consequence a person
   feels, not a buzzword.
5. **One plain identity line** — "I run GnK, a software boutique that builds custom
   software and AI systems." / "I'm the founder of Wapahki in Toronto." Name the
   product only when the campaign-specific playbook supplies a concrete,
   non-jargon explanation that helps the recipient understand the question.
6. **State the offer at its real maturity.** An existing product is described in
   the present tense as something that works now. Discovery work can be framed as
   exploratory. Describe the operational outcome, not a pile of technology.
7. **Invite them to correct you** — "Because you see this process firsthand / oversee
   [X], I'd like to understand where the delays actually occur and whether this would
   solve a meaningful problem for [company]."
8. **One primary CTA plus an optional referral escape.** A direct owner may get a
   20-minute conversation ask followed by a referral option. A routing contact
   gets only the referral ask. Do not stack a call, a referral, and an email
   response in the same close.
9. **Signature** — the campaign's real sender line.

**Tune to the role.** The same problem gets a different angle per person: the
front-line assistant is asked what they *see firsthand*; a CISO/IT-governance owner
is asked what makes the sensitive handoff *hard to digitize securely*; a planning
director is asked what *throws the schedule off*. Change the question and the framing
to the recipient's remit — never send the same body to two roles.

**Approved reference (GnK, funeral death-registration):**

> Hi Justin,
> Once Arbor Memorial has collected everything required for a death registration,
> how much manual work is involved in getting it accepted by the province?
> I suspect the difficult part is finding incomplete or inconsistent information
> only after the registration has already moved between the people
> responsible for completing it. That creates more follow-up for funeral home staff
> and can leave families waiting weeks before they can move forward with an estate or
> access survivor benefits.
> I run GnK, a software boutique that builds custom software and AI systems. We're
> exploring a digital workflow that checks a death registration before submission and
> securely moves it through to the registry…
> Because you see this process firsthand, I'd like to understand where the delays
> actually occur and whether this would solve a meaningful problem for Arbor Memorial.
> Would you be open to a 20-minute conversation next week? If another team owns
> this more directly, a referral would be helpful.
> Andrew

Same problem, re-tuned for the CISO/GRC director: open on *"how much of the delay
comes from exchanging sensitive information across organizations that don't share one
system,"* and close by asking *what prevents this process from being digitized today*.
Match the question to what that person actually owns.

## Default follow-up pattern (T2–T4)

This timing and job pattern applies to campaigns that retain the default
seven-touch plan. A campaign-specific override controls when present; Wapahki's
seven-touch early-discovery sequence is defined in
`wapahki.md`, and OutageHub's four-email current-decision-to-discovery sequence
is defined in `outagehub.md`.

Follow-ups are not reminders. **Each one does ONE new job and can stand on its
own.** Never write "just following up", "circling back", "bumping this", "in case
you missed it", or a fake deadline. If you have nothing new to add, don't send it.

**The follow-up value gate (hard rule).** Reject any follow-up that does not add
new evidence, a more concrete example, a useful artifact or a materially
different ask. A follow-up must pass one of those four and must not restate a
prior touch's hypothesis, question, or CTA. Do not grow more confident than the
evidence across the sequence: more touches never turn a hypothesis into an
assertion. Full detail is in `docs/outbound-doctrine/` (handbook D).

The active channel plan is fixed: **T2 is a day-4 email in the T1 thread, T3 is
a day-6 LinkedIn connection request, and T4 is a day-9 email.** T2 and T4 are
shorter than T1. T3 is only a connection note, not a full message.

### T2 — first follow-up: give them something useful
Lead with a NEW, concrete thing, not a re-ask. In order of strength:

1. **An artifact — the strongest T2 when it already exists.** Offer or bring something real and specific
   to them:
   - **GnK:** a one-screen mockup, or a precise regulatory/technical hook (e.g.
     "the EEM report is due March 31; the MDMER limit check is the manual part").
   - **GnK operational problem:** a one-screen mockup, or the cost reframed for a DIFFERENT
     consequence than T1 (if T1 hit analyst hours, T2 hits penalty exposure or
     trapped cash).
2. **An assumption-led cost model — the default when the unit economics are
   visible.** Turn the operational burden from T1 into a small equation the
   recipient can correct. For example, people x hours per week x weeks x a
   loaded hourly rate, locations x checking time, or affected users x stalled
   rate x recoverable actions. This is analysis, not company research.
   - Start with `if`, `suppose`, or equally clear assumption language.
   - Show the inputs and the resulting hours, cost, or actions in the email.
   - Use round numbers. Never make an illustrative input sound measured.
   - Calculate one base burden. A second value is allowed only for a distinct
     consequence, such as claim exposure or lost high-intent actions. Never add
     uncertain risk exposure to labour cost as if both are guaranteed savings.
   - End with one calibration question: is the order of magnitude right, or does
     most of the cost or effort sit elsewhere?
   - Use this only when the recipient owns or can see the economics and T1 or
     verified account context supplies a credible unit of work or public scale.
     Do not force it for a frontline recipient, a sensitive safety scenario, or
     a workflow that cannot be reduced to honest inputs.
3. **A new observation** grounded in something learned since (a hiring post, a
   public number, a regulation, a recent event).
4. **A sharper, lower-bar question** ("even a one-line 'we handle this with X'
   would tell me whether this is worth your time").

Close with the same low-friction ask + referral escape.

### T3 — LinkedIn connection request
Write the note as a connection request that has not yet been accepted. In one
short sentence, name the real topic Andrew is researching and why this person's
work is relevant. Do not ask for a call, paste the email, explain the whole
product, include a link or signature, or say `I emailed you`. Never write as if
the connection already exists. The hard ceiling is 300 characters; shorter is
usually better.

### T4 — return to email with a second source or angle
Add one new, role-relevant lesson, artifact, or concrete view of the proposed
process. It must be distinct from T1 and T2 and useful even if the reader never
accepts the LinkedIn request. When a campaign reserves a particular interview
or source pack for T4, use that source and keep any attribution or evidence
caveat in the campaign file. Ask one narrow question. If the new material does
not fit this person, ask who owns the issue or close the thread cleanly rather
than forcing the analogy. No guilt and no `last chance`.

**Never in a follow-up:** stack the whole product; restate T1; use
"quick/just/circling/bumping/touch base"; add pressure or a fake deadline; send one
with nothing new.

**Approved reference — GnK T2 (assumption-led cost model):**

> Hi Nataliya,
>
> I tried to put a number around the project-controls burden.
>
> If four people spend 10 hours a week for eight weeks rebuilding the schedule
> history and locating supporting records, that is roughly 320 hours. At a
> blended cost of $125 an hour, the reconstruction alone costs about $40,000.
>
> Aecon's numbers may be very different. Is this the right order of magnitude,
> or does most of the effort happen elsewhere?
>
> Thanks,
> Andrew Gordienko
> GnK

Match the logic, not the wording or numbers. The operating unit must come from
the recipient's T1 or verified context, and every numeric input remains an
explicit assumption until the recipient confirms it.

## Active second-touchpoint (T2) refinements

- Choose the T2 job by the strength of the new material, not by the desire to send another message. Use an artifact only when it is genuinely specific, useful without a meeting, and relevant to the recipient’s remit. Use a new observation only when it is verified and creates a distinct reason to ask or offer something. Use the lower-bar question when the important unknown is narrow enough for an ordinary one-line reply. Brief, directly relevant proof can support the original outcome, but must not imply the same result here. [T001] [T002] [T006] [T009] [T014] [T016]

- An artifact is not a brochure recast as value. Name the concrete thing, what it shows or lets the recipient assess, and why that matters in their work. Keep it available under the project’s no-link rule; do not make the recipient navigate elsewhere to discover its value. [T009] [T014]

- A new observation must remain a fact, not a diagnosis. State the observable trigger or constraint, then make only the bounded, role-relevant implication that it supports. Do not use surface personalization as the new reason to write. [T009] [T016]

- For the lower-bar route, ask one answerable question that reduces uncertainty about ownership, workflow, or relevance. It should be easier to answer than T1’s call request, not a disguised request to evaluate the whole offer. A routing answer is useful when ownership is genuinely unclear. [T001] [T002] [T009] [T014]

- Keep the day-4 T2 timing as the active default. The cited recommendations range from roughly one to three days and do not establish a universal best interval, so do not compress the gap merely because a source proposes a faster cadence. Any approved cadence experiment should compare segments with similar seniority and inbox pressure while holding the audience and T2 job constant. [T001] [T002] [T006] [T007] [T010] [T016] [T018]

- Reply in the original thread and retain its subject at T2. Use one short context-restoring phrase about the T1 topic, then move immediately to the new material. Do not imply that the recipient should have seen, reread, or acted on the first email, and do not frame silence as a debt. [T001] [T002] [T009] [T014]

- T2 testing should compare artifact-led, observation-led, and lower-bar-question variants within the same ICP, same-thread format, and timing window. Change one variable at a time and assess inboxing, reply quality, and downstream outcomes separately. Treat claims about reply, booking, or conversion lifts as unproven tests rather than benchmarks. [T002] [T006] [T009] [T014] [T016] [T018]

## Active founder & creator sales-clip refinements

Distilled from three practitioner video clips (Cameron Zoub/Whop [C1], Shelby
Sapp [C2], Chris Donnelly/Searchable [C3]). Apply these as writing tests and
safeguards. They sharpen how a message reads; they never relax the evidence,
role-fit, single-CTA, no-link, or deliverability gates above, which win on any
conflict. Reported reply, conversion, and revenue numbers in these clips are
promotional and unverified. Never quote them and never treat them as targets.

- **Diagnose, do not demo.** Prefer opening with a specific, useful observation
  over asking for a demo or a call up front. Bring what you noticed and what you
  suspect, then offer to talk it through. The observation must be source-grounded
  or a clearly-labelled hypothesis the recipient can correct, never an invented
  internal fact. Deliver the finding in the body; the no-link rule still forbids
  attaching or linking a document in a cold touch. [C3]

- **Frame value in the recipient's terms, concretely.** Lead with the one thing
  that actually annoys this role, in their words, not with what Andrew finds
  clever about the product. One honest everyday analogy can make an operational
  cost land faster than jargon, as long as it stays truthful and does not imply a
  measured claim. [C1]

- **Say it the way you would out loud.** Draft as if you had 20 seconds to tell
  this person at a bar, or record a quick voice memo and write from how you
  actually said it. If a line sounds like an email and not like a person talking,
  rewrite it. Never write "reaching out on behalf of." [C1]

- **Reduce decision load; give one clear next step.** A stalled reader defaults
  to "just send it over, I'll think about it," which is a soft no. The fix is
  usually more clarity and one obvious next step, not more options or a vaguer,
  softer ask. Make the single CTA a concrete, easy action. This means clarity,
  not pressure: keep the calm easy-out, never presume a booking or invent
  urgency, and an honest routing question is still a valid win. [C2]

- **Stand out with real, one-to-one effort, never a template.** Generic,
  clearly-automated messages do not earn replies; specific genuine relevance
  does. This is not a licence for stunts, invasive contact, false urgency, or
  fabricated context, all of which remain banned. Effort shows as a real reason
  this exact person is worth writing to. [C1]

## Outbound doctrine (the four focused handbooks)

Consolidated must-follow rules distilled from ten practitioner talks into four
handbooks in `docs/outbound-doctrine/` (A research & proof boundaries, B
problem-hypothesis & ownership testing, C cold-email construction, D sequence
progression & account routing). These exist to stop seven recurring generator
failures. They are disciplined defaults to apply; the house rules above win on
conflict, and no reported reply/conversion statistic is ever quoted as a
forecast.

### The two governing hard rules

1. **Evidence boundary.** Public evidence may establish the company's
   environment, but it cannot establish the private workflow, its owner, its
   difficulty or its financial consequence. (Gate 1.)
2. **Follow-up value gate.** Reject any follow-up that does not add new evidence,
   a more concrete example, a useful artifact or a materially different ask.

### A — Research and proof boundaries

- Classify every research item as an observed fact or an inferred relevance; a
  found fact is not self-evidently relevant, so hook it to what the role is
  measured on or drop it.
- Only the situation (environment) may be treated as confirmed from public
  evidence. Workflow, owner, difficulty, and cost start unconfirmed.
- Quote the person's own words, or mark the inference ("I'd imagine…", "not sure
  if…", "correct me if I'm wrong"). Never dress company-level news as something
  the individual personally feels.
- Anchor the reason for writing to an observed action, not a title. Use only
  real, sourced numbers, framed as industry benchmarks, never as a measured claim
  about this account. Scope every proof point to exactly what it supports.
- Never invent operational language; use the recipient's own words. Compliments
  are not evidence.

### B — Problem-hypothesis and ownership testing

- State the problem as a falsifiable hypothesis with a self-check, not a
  diagnosis. Use uncertainty language on anything private.
- Follow the SPICED order (Situation → Pain → Impact → Critical event →
  Decision). A cold email touches only Situation (evidenced) + one Pain
  hypothesis. Never assert Impact, a dollar/ROI/hours figure, a Critical Event, or
  a Decision process cold; jumping Situation → Impact is the classic tell.
- Do not infer ownership from a title; ask or route.
- Ground the question in the last real occurrence, not a hypothetical or a
  "would you use…". Withhold solution, product, pilot, demo, and peer-story until
  a reply confirms the problem.
- Offer a bounded choice, answerable from memory in one line, with "no" made
  easy. Fast disqualification is a win.

### C — Cold-email construction

- Order: reason (about them, an observed fact or a question) → one Pain
  hypothesis → value/light proof if real → one CTA. Positioning before product:
  frame the problem and one real alternative before naming the offer.
- Open about the recipient, never the sender or product. Roughly 50–100 words,
  3–4 sentences, short mobile-readable blocks. Exactly one ask.
- CTA is offer- or interest-based, never a meeting/time request in a cold first
  touch and never a long open-ended question. Translate any feature to its value
  ("so what?") or cut it. Attach every number to a cause.
- No FOMO or "falling behind" pressure. Read every line aloud; if you would not
  say it to the person, cut it.

### D — Sequence progression and account routing

- Prepare at least three distinct premises up front so each touch rotates to a
  new angle. Each touch advances the stage or switches angle and still leads with
  the reason and one CTA. Do not fade; end with an honest close and honor it.
- Pick the first contact by likely problem ownership, not seniority.
  Single-thread first when the owner is unconfirmed. Never send two contacts at
  one account the same body; match the ask to the role (operator confirms the
  workflow, router points inward, economic owner speaks to consequence/budget).
- Ask a routing question as a correctable guess ("looks like your team owns X —
  you, or someone else?"), not an open "who owns this?". Reach a second
  stakeholder only after a first contact confirms the problem and points there.

### Pre-send checklist (any "no" is a revise or do_not_contact)

1. Is every account claim a cited public fact or explicitly a hypothesis?
2. Is any private workflow/owner/difficulty/dollar figure asserted from public
   evidence alone?
3. Is ownership asked or routed rather than inferred from a title?
4. Does the main question point at a real, specific situation and stay answerable
   in one line?
5. Are solution, pilot, ROI, and quantified impact absent until a reply confirms
   the problem?
6. One recipient-first ask, offer/interest-based, ≤ ~100 words, no banned
   buzzwords, no FOMO?
7. For a follow-up: does it pass the value gate and avoid repeating a prior
   hypothesis, question, or CTA?
8. For a multi-contact account: is each body distinct and role-matched, not a
   blast?
