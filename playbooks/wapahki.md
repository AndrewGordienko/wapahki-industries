# Wapahki early B2B discovery

**Sender:** Andrew Gordienko  
**Signature line:** Founder, Wapahki Industries

## Current posture

Wapahki is an early-stage robotics company in Toronto. It is not selling
equipment yet. Andrew is speaking with people close to manufacturing,
packaging, warehouse, quality, engineering, and related work to learn which
repetitive handling tasks are genuinely worth automating and which look
repetitive but are not.

Say plainly what Wapahki is building so the reader can picture it: a robotic
cell that takes a single repeated pick-and-place or transfer motion off a
production line — one product, picked up and placed (or moved) the same way
every cycle. Describing the machine in one plain sentence is orientation and is
required in touch 1. It is not a pitch and must never tip into selling.

Cold outreach is market discovery, not product qualification. Naming what the
machine does is allowed; do NOT write as if Wapahki already has an installed
system, a candidate project at that company, a specific manual task you have
confirmed, or a sales process the recipient has entered.

Never use these ideas in Wapahki discovery copy:

- technical screen, fit screen, automation screen, qualification, or
  disqualification;
- reject a weak opportunity;
- deployment, pilot scope, paid pilot, or site assessment;
- required rate, pickup, placement, and ordinary exceptions as a premature
  specification bundle; or
- a one-page sketch or other artifact that Andrew has not actually produced.

Do not say a recipient owns a process, makes a decision, is responsible for it,
or is accountable for it unless verified evidence establishes that fact. A
title can establish a useful vantage point. It does not establish authority.

## Contact tracks

Not every contact gets the same message. Classify each by role and match the
track. Only people who could realistically evaluate or champion the problem earn
a full sequence.

- **Operational** (plant, operations, production, warehouse, maintenance,
  engineering, quality, planning, packaging, floor): the seven-touch discovery
  below. Lead with the concrete physical job and ask what makes it hard in
  reality. Write like a builder talking to an operator, not a researcher running
  a framework.
- **Economic** (finance, controller, accounting): a seven-touch conversation
  framed as equipment economics, not floor operations. Ask how the business case
  for flexible automation across mixed, short-run programs is judged — payback on
  one program versus across several — and offer a route to operations.
- **Routing** (design, sales, admin, dispatch, and any role clearly outside the
  operational or economic remit): ONE honest email. Name what Wapahki builds, say
  you are researching how the physical work is done, acknowledge it is likely
  outside their role, and ask who owns that process. Ask for a name, not a call.
  Do not run a seven-touch campaign at a routing contact.

## Touch 1 structure (operational track)

The first email should read like a builder talking to an operator, not a
researcher running a framework. Four moves:

1. Lead with the concrete physical work that tends to stay manual on this kind of
   line — moving finished cases, building or breaking down pallets, transferring
   a product between two points, resetting after a changeover — chosen to fit
   this company and role.
2. Name in one plain sentence that Wapahki is building a flexible robotic cell
   that performs that repeated motion: it picks up and places (or moves) one
   product the same way every cycle.
3. Ask the real question: does that work stay roughly the same across products
   and runs, or do changes in weight, size, pallet pattern, or format make every
   run substantially different — and what makes it difficult in reality?
4. Ask for a 20-minute call. One ask, one line.

Do NOT open with "as [title], you would know"; do NOT recite that Wapahki is
early stage and not selling yet as a disclaimer; do NOT add "an email reply would
also help." Convey that Wapahki is early by being honest about the stage of the
work — "before we build too far, we're trying to understand whether this is worth
automating" — not by a disclaimer sentence.

Name what the machine does in plain words, but do not assert that a specific
named task — sealed-case palletizing, case loading, or another motion convenient
for a robot — already exists here. Public evidence that a company warehouses,
packs, manufactures, or distributes products does not prove that any specific
task exists or is manual. Describe the machine; ask, don't assume, whether a task
like it exists on their floor.

## Seven-touch discovery sequence (operational track)

Seven touches over roughly four weeks. Stop on any reply and continue as a
normal conversation.

| Touch | Day | Channel | One job |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | email | Lead with the candidate physical job, name the robotic cell, ask whether the work stays consistent across runs and what makes it hard. Ask for a 20-minute call. |
| 2 | 5 | email, same thread | Add one new concrete point about the same job (a specific changeover, the reset around the repeated motion, one work area where it stays identical) and ask if it holds on their floor. |
| 3 | 9 | LinkedIn connection | Connect in one plain sentence about the specific handling work you're researching. |
| 4 | 15 | email, new thread | Share, in plain words, one thing you've started to believe (e.g. the best first jobs stay in one work area and one pack family while people keep handling the odd cases) and ask whether it matches their operation. |
| 5 | 20 | email, same thread as touch 4 | Ask plainly whether this person is closest to the question or whether another function knows the physical work better. |
| 6 | 26 | LinkedIn message | State one concrete thing you've concluded and ask one question answerable in a line. No meeting ask. |
| 7 | 32 | email, new thread | Close the specific handling thread calmly and leave the door open. |

Each touch carries ONE genuinely new concrete point — a specific changeover, a
named work area or pack family, a distinct kind of variation, a real cost of
doing it by hand — and never re-asks touch 1's question in reworded form.

Write like a builder: plain, specific, confident. Do NOT use abstract framing
such as "where repeatable physical handling ends and a new set of checks begins"
— say the actual thing ("does this case-handling job stay the same when the
product changes?"). Do not hedge with "I may be combining two steps" or "I may be
asking this from the wrong angle." Do not retell Wapahki after touch 1, ask for
repeated calls, or run a sales process.

### Touch 2: sharpen the same job

Stay on the exact job from touch 1 and add one concrete detail — the reset around
the repeated motion, a specific changeover, or one work area where the motion
stays identical — then ask one easy question about whether it holds on their
floor. Keep it in plain floor language, not a process-distinction exercise.

### Touch 3: LinkedIn connection

Keep the pre-acceptance note under 200 characters, in plain builder language
about the specific handling work you're researching. Do not ask for a call,
include a link or signature, paste the email, or imply the recipient read it.

### Touch 4: what you've started to believe

Open a new thread. In plain words, share one thing these conversations have led
you to believe — for example that the best first jobs stay within one work area
and one pack family while people keep handling the odd cases — and ask whether it
matches their operation. Do not label it "hypothesis," do not claim the task
exists, and do not offer a sketch or artifact.

### Touch 5: check the route

Stay in touch 4's thread. Ask plainly whether this person is closest to the
physical-handling question or whether a named function knows the work better.
Suitable functions include warehouse process improvement, engineering,
operations, production, packaging, quality, and continuous improvement.

The question is about where insight sits, not who has authority. Do not ask who
conducts an initial automation or technical review.

### Touch 6: test the synthesis

Write a short LinkedIn direct message as if connected. State one synthesis in
plain language and ask exactly one question the role could answer in a line.
Do not ask for a meeting or introduce equipment, a pilot, a sketch, or a new
process theory.

### Touch 7: close

Name the specific handling question in plain words and close the thread without
guilt, urgency, another meeting request, or another routing request. Leave the
door open to a later reply.

## Subject plan

- Touch 1 uses a naturally capitalized, two-to-five-word subject naming the
  operational question.
- Touch 2 repeats touch 1 exactly.
- Touch 4 opens a new hypothesis thread with a distinct subject.
- Touch 5 repeats touch 4 exactly.
- Touch 7 uses a third, distinct closing subject.
- Touches 3 and 6 have no subject.

Avoid `Automation`, `Quick question`, `Following up`, `Technical screen`, or
anything that sounds like an internal project stage.

## Role-specific vantage points

- **Plant, operations, or general management:** sees which work recurs across
  shifts or products and where priorities or constraints differ by area.
- **Production or site management:** sees the difference between a repeated
  handling step and the product changes, stops, staffing, or timing around it.
- **Continuous improvement or process roles:** sees where superficially similar
  work becomes a different process and which observations are measurable.
- **Maintenance or engineering:** sees which stops are routine, which recovery
  requires judgement, and which physical or safety constraints outsiders miss.
- **Warehouse, logistics, or material handling:** sees which movements repeat
  across shifts and which change with order mix, storage condition, customer,
  or carrier requirements.
- **Packaging:** sees which product, material, pack, or customer change alters
  the physical handling process.
- **Quality, regulatory, or sanitation:** sees when a change triggers different
  inspection, line-clearance, traceability, sanitation, or release controls.
- **Planning or supply chain:** sees which handoffs repeat across schedules and
  which are created by demand, inventory, or packaging variation.
- **Commercial or customer-program roles:** sees which customer requirements,
  pack counts, artwork, or seasonal programs change the physical work. Ask
  only what they can observe, then seek orientation to operations if useful.
- **Finance or administration:** sees how unfamiliar ideas enter the company
  and which function would be closest to the operating question. Do not ask
  for technical or floor-level judgement.
- **Operators or technicians:** see setup, lifting, clearing, resets, and
  everyday exceptions. Do not imply buying authority.
