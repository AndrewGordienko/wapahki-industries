# GnK problem-led software and AI engineering

**Sender:** Andrew Gordienko  
**Signature company:** GnK

## Offer

GnK is a small senior product, software and AI engineering team in Toronto. GnK
starts from a specific, source-backed operational or technical problem and takes
on the focused build when an organization lacks the time, capacity or specialist
experience to ship it internally.

The normal commercial entry point, after qualification, is a fixed-fee paid
pilot with a defined historical-data test and customer-controlled decisions.
Cold outreach must not sell that pilot, quote pricing or imply that a product
has already been designed. It earns evidence and the correct owner. Payment and
implementation mechanics belong in the later proposal, never the no-reply
sequence. Never promise pay-on-delivery or free development.

## Posture

Hypothesis-led enterprise sales. The commercial path is:

`evidence → problem hypothesis → correct owner → discovery → quantified case → paid pilot → expansion`

Cold outreach does not sell software or an imagined platform. It tests whether
one costly, recurring workflow exists and finds the person who owns it. GnK
designs the solution only after the workflow is confirmed.

Research broadly across a cohort, contact narrowly inside each account,
validate before quantifying, and stop when the evidence fails. Run only one or
two workflow hypotheses at a time across 30–50 similar companies. Do not invent
a different product for every account.

GnK is not already building a "scenario agent" for a cold prospect. `Scenario
agent`, `scenario sandbox`, `fan-narrative agent`, and similar labels are banned
from buyer-facing copy. Do not write `I'm building an agent` unless a real
product is already being built.

Describe the proposed tool through a person doing a concrete thing. For example:
`a small internal tool that runs one scoring-rule change against sample cases
before the live system changes`. A proposed tool is one example GnK could scope
after learning the workflow, not an existing GnK product.

Do not ask broad questions such as "where would you put AI?" That asks the
recipient to invent GnK's offer. Bring a considered hypothesis and let the
recipient correct it.

Do not use peer activity as vague FOMO. The NBA hiring for automated officiating
does not prove that Athletes Unlimited should build officiating AI, that it can
do so better, or that AI belongs "in the product." If used at all, it is only
evidence that leagues are investing engineering resources in live competition
systems.

## Qualification

Pursue a workflow hypothesis only when all seven conditions are credible:

- it repeats frequently
- poor handling creates material time, money, error or risk
- the consequence can be measured
- records the company already has could answer the question
- an identifiable department owns the work
- historical data can test it within 30–45 days
- a successful first engagement could justify roughly $40k–$90k

Every account thesis must keep these seven fields separate:

1. **Observed fact:** what a direct public source actually says.
2. **Hypothesis:** the recurring operating problem that may exist.
3. **Owner:** the role or department that performs or controls the work.
4. **Consequence:** the time, money, error or risk created by delay or failure.
5. **Records:** the documents or systems that could contain the answer.
6. **Pilot:** how historical work could test the hypothesis without operational
   risk.
7. **Kill condition:** the finding that would make GnK stop pursuing it.

Never let an observed fact silently become proof of an internal problem. A lead
is qualified only after discovery confirms a recurring workflow, measurable
consequence, named owner, accessible data, credible champion and defined pilot
outcome. If two or more are missing after discovery, pause the opportunity.

If the only research is an old case study plus a researcher-invented `ai_project`,
return `do_not_contact` until a current, role-relevant signal is found. Do not
use smoother writing to hide missing timing. If the role does not have a
credible route to the project, return `do_not_contact`.

## Owner independence and business transition

GnK may target established owner-operated businesses when a current public
business signal supports it. This route is about operating continuity and
freeing a bottlenecked senior person from routine work. It is not demographic
targeting.

Valid signals include a company-announced succession or ownership transfer, a
new president/GM/COO, an owner publicly seeking operating leadership, an
acquisition integration, a modernization initiative, repeated hiring around a
documented workflow bottleneck, or a directly disclosed knowledge-transfer
problem.

Never infer or mention a recipient's age, generation, retirement plan, health,
wealth, desire to sell or family situation. `Baby boomer business`, `older
owner`, `before you retire`, `succession crisis`, `legacy business` and `your
business depends on you` are banned from cold copy. A founder/owner title alone
does not prove owner dependence.

Bring one bounded, source-backed workflow hypothesis:

- prepare an estimate from an incoming email, drawings or PDFs for human review
- assemble a dispatch plan or routine customer update from existing job records
- match invoices, purchase orders and exceptions for a controller to approve
- retrieve the right SOP and prior service history for a manager handling an
  exception
- prepare a clean handoff so a manager can act without waiting for one expert

Do not bundle several workflows or offer to automate the whole business. The
pilot should keep human approval for financial, legal, safety, employment,
credit, tax and customer commitments. Useful measures include senior-person
interruptions, turnaround time, backlog, rework and manager escalations.

For companies roughly 20–250 people, an Owner/President can be a valid economic
buyer. Other routes include General Manager, COO, VP/Director Operations,
Controller, Service Manager, Dispatch Manager or Estimating Manager. An
accountant, succession advisor, M&A broker or fractional executive is a referral
channel; do not claim their client has a problem.

The first touch should ask who handles the concrete decision when the event
occurs and name the specific idea GnK is exploring. Keep the internal problem
credible, not hedged. No cold meeting ask; discovery is earned by a reply, not
scheduled in a no-reply thread. Never open on `AI`, sale valuation, staff
replacement or the owner's personal plans.

## Touch 1 standard

A GnK first touch is written entirely from the recipient's perspective, not as
an internal deal memo. It should include one verified fact or source-backed
reason for choosing the organization and one concrete, company-specific question
about the recipient's real workflow.

The strongest opening question does not restate a hypothesis; it exposes the
actual process without pretending to understand it. The shape is: when a specific
triggering event happens, what happens between the resulting record or report and
the decision it should drive? For example: "When a bridge or major-culvert
inspection identifies a condition requiring attention, what happens between the
completed inspection report and the decision to repair, restrict, monitor, or
escalate it?" That is recognizable and answerable in one sentence.

Never copy the private hypothesis phrasing into the email. Wording like "The
question is whether…" is internal memo language and must not appear. Re-express
the hypothesis as a plain question the recipient would actually receive.

**Qualify before you pitch.** The cold sequence's job is to establish three
things before any screen, tool, or historical pilot is ever mentioned: whether
the handoff actually exists, who owns it, and whether an existing system already
handles it end to end. Do not design or name a product in cold copy. Do not start
sketching a screen before a broken handoff is confirmed. The account's real need
could be inspection capture, compliance scheduling, defect prioritization, or
remediation tracking — which one is a discovery outcome, not an opening
assumption. A good touch 1 says something like "I'm trying to establish whether
that handoff creates real work before assuming you need another tool."

The deal context may contain more than one candidate product. Never let a
product line from the notes (for example a mobile inspection system or a
compliance-cycle enforcer) turn the qualifying question into a pitch. If the
recipient replies "one system already handles that end to end," that is a
successful, welcome outcome — invite it explicitly.

Do not invent anything the evidence does not establish. That includes ownership
(a title is a routing hypothesis, not proof the person runs the workflow day to
day), workflow specifics (do not assert a "post-storm inspection" or a "corporate
escalation" step that is not in the record), and named colleagues (never suggest
"ask Karin" or route to a specific person unless that person's relevance is
independently established). Do not ask for a metric ("how long does it take")
before the task's existence is confirmed. Do not describe a finished system or
assert cost in touch 1.

The recipient should be able to reply `yes`, `no`, `we already do that`, or
`speak to this person` without doing unpaid product discovery for GnK. If the
email's only reason to reply is curiosity about an undefined AI label, reject it.
End email touches with:

```
Thanks,
Andrew Gordienko
GnK
```

## Cost framing

Define the cost instead of decorating the email with a large annual estimate.
When the supplied research contains a public figure or a usable unit of work,
build a short model from explicit inputs such as people, hours, frequency,
loaded rate, delayed value, or high-intent actions. Show the arithmetic in plain
English and keep all unpublished inputs visibly hypothetical.

Do not use a quantified model anywhere in the four cold touches or treat silence
as confirmation. Do not invent an annual figure, ROI or recoverable savings.
Build the transparent equation only in a reply-based branch after discovery has
established frequency, units and a credible owner. Do not treat contingent
penalties, claims or revenue as expected cash.

## Buying group

Map five functions before broadening outreach:

- **Operator:** performs the work and can reconstruct a recent case.
- **Process owner:** is accountable for the workflow and its outcome.
- **Economic buyer:** can approve the first engagement.
- **Technical/security owner:** controls data access and deployment.
- **Router:** can identify the roles above but must not be sold to.

The first three outreach contacts at an account should be the strongest
operator, process-owner and economic or technical routes, not simply the first
three people found. A router receives one initial message and at most one
follow-up. Then stop. Never turn a router into a four-touch prospect.

## Role-tiered qualifying sequence

Cold outreach qualifies the workflow before it ever proposes a tool, and its
length is tiered by how directly the contact touches the work. Do not contact all
three at once; sequence them, strongest operator first, and use the others only
if the operator does not resolve ownership.

- **Direct operator / process owner — full four touches** over roughly three
  weeks (days 1, 4, 9, 18):
  1. **Does the handoff exist:** ask whether the report itself moves the work
     forward, or whether someone separately reconciles the finding with earlier
     assessments and decides the follow-up. Offer to discuss the last completed
     case. No product, no metric.
  2. **Does an existing system already handle it:** note what is already in
     place, then ask who reviewed, decided, and recorded completion on the last
     real case. Say plainly that "one existing system already handles it" is an
     equally useful answer.
  3. **Connect:** a short LinkedIn note on the same question.
  4. **Who owns it:** ask which group owns the decision, offering plausible
     generic roles (never a named person), then stop.
- **Adjacent role — two qualification touches.** Approach only through the angle
  they genuinely own, ask whether the workflow even enters their remit, and route
  if it does not. Never assume it belongs to them.
- **Executive / router — one fallback email.** Ask for the right job title. Do
  not pretend they operate the process, and do not send it in parallel with the
  operator sequence.

Each touch must add information, never restate the last. The strongest opening
shape is:

`When [specific event] happens, is the report enough to move the work forward, or does someone separately reconcile it and decide the follow-up?`

**The fundamental copy rule.** Internal research should make the email more
specific, but internal uncertainty, qualification language, and proof boundaries
must never appear in buyer-facing copy. Research goes in the spoken brief, not
the email. Do not write "the source does not prove", "not yet established",
"only after qualification", "operating task/owner", or hedges like "I am not
assuming". Ask the qualifying question plainly; do not pitch a product to fill
the space where discovery belongs.

**Differentiate by role; never send three near-identical emails.** The three
contacts at an account occupy different positions and must be approached
differently:

- **Direct operator / process owner:** ask about the actual workflow — what
  happens between the triggering record and the decision it drives.
- **Adjacent role:** approach only through the angle they genuinely own. A
  preparedness or emergency-readiness contact is asked how condition records are
  used after a wildfire, flood, or access event — not about routine inspection
  scheduling.
- **Executive or router:** ask for routing in the first email. Do not pretend
  they operate the process day to day.

If the copy for two contacts at the same company is interchangeable, it is wrong.

A positive reply leads to discovery around one recent case: trigger, first
observer, steps, systems and documents, delay, frequency, consequence, owner,
prior attempts and the conditions for a paid pilot. Quantify only after those
facts are learned.

## Paid pilot and expansion

Sell a bounded historical-data pilot, not a vague transformation. Keep live
decisions with the customer. A typical first engagement reviews completed or
abandoned cases, compares GnK's output with the historical outcome, and measures
time saved, money found, errors avoided or risk reduced over 30–45 days. The
normal range is $40k–$90k, with a narrower $40k–$75k range appropriate for a
chargeback evidence pilot.

Only offer the pilot after discovery confirms the workflow, consequence, owner,
data, champion and outcome. The scope must name the historical sample,
deliverables, acceptance criteria, responsibilities, timeline, price, customer
approval boundary, kill condition and what expansion would mean if the pilot
succeeds.

## Hypothesis-led T1 gate

Before writing a GnK first touch, complete these statements privately:

1. `The public evidence establishes ______, but does not establish ______.`
2. `When ______ happens, this role may have to decide or reconstruct ______.`
3. `The records likely checked are ______, but access is not yet confirmed.`
4. `The opportunity stops if ______.`

If any blank remains abstract, the email is not ready. Do not define a proposed
tool before the operating task is confirmed. The message must make the
evidence boundary clear and ask one answerable question about the event,
decision and information needed.

Apply these additional gates:

- Start from the recipient's remit. A commercial leader normally cares about
  adoption, proof of value, renewals, expansion, and getting customer feedback
  into product decisions. Do not force a scientific-user workflow onto a
  commercial title.
- Do not set a product boundary in cold copy. Whether a later solution is an
  internal tool, customer-facing capability or no build at all is a discovery
  outcome, not an assumption.
- Do not assume access to customer records, product telemetry, scientific
  evidence, plant data, or other systems. Name only information the public
  evidence supports, or say that the concept would use information the company
  already captures and is permitted to use.
- Define the failure precisely. `Weak experiment`, `weak prior attempt`, and
  `likely repeat` are not explanations. Name the target, material, condition,
  contradictory result, failed check, or other concrete reason the prior work
  matters.
- Describe support, not scientific or operational certainty. Prefer `surfaces
  comparable prior experiments and the evidence against repeating them for
  review` over `identifies likely repeats`.
- Do not catalogue inputs. A sentence listing chemistry, exposure, reactor,
  curing, plant and lab data still leaves the product unclear. Name the few
  inputs needed for one decision, then say exactly what the system produces.
- Prefer one open bottleneck question when writing to a founder, technical
  leader, or experienced operator. Use multiple-choice questions only when the
  choices are source-backed, mutually exclusive and make the question easier to
  answer. Do not guess three possible bottlenecks merely to sound specific.
- Tie value to the operating constraint. For site deployment and commissioning,
  useful outcomes include a shorter commissioning cycle and less repeated
  involvement from scarce senior technical people. Do not retreat to a vague
  claim about better data.
